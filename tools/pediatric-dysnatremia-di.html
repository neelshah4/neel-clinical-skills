Dysnatremia and diabetes insipidus at the bedside

Work out which pattern the child is in; then run the vasopressin infusion if it is central diabetes insipidus. Where the inputs cannot settle a question, the tool says so and names the test that would.

Educational tool — not for clinical decision-making. Not a diagnostic device, not FDA-cleared, not validated. The management tab assumes central diabetes insipidus and nothing else. Do not enter protected health information.
Scope, evidence base and storage

The management tab does not cover nephrogenic diabetes insipidus, and the diagnostic tab keeps nephrogenic DI in view at equal prominence until a desmopressin response is entered, because no input on that tab separates the two. There is no adequately powered pediatric diagnostic-accuracy study for central DI, SIADH or cerebral salt wasting; most thresholds shown here are adult-derived or come from pediatric cohorts of a few tens of patients, and each carries its population tag and its n where it appears. No desmopressin dose appears anywhere in this tool. Defer to clinical judgment, the treating team, your endocrinology and nephrology colleagues, and local protocols. Every value stays in this browser session, is never saved and is never transmitted; nothing but the color-theme preference is written to this device.

What the inputs support

    Run and titrate the infusion

    This tab assumes central diabetes insipidus. It has no logic for nephrogenic DI; that exclusion lives on the Interpret tab and needs a desmopressin response. Doses are in mU/kg/hr throughout.

    Titrate on urine specific gravity, then urine output

    In the only pediatric cohort that measured which parameter tracks the dose (14 PICU children, 1997), specific gravity correlated with the vasopressin dose at r = 0.96, p = 0.009, and urine output at r = 0.93, p = 0.02. Neither serum sodium nor serum osmolality tracked the dose at all. PMID 9467135

    Why specific gravity is the parameter here, and not on the Interpret tab

    On that tab osmolality is the measurement and specific gravity is a proxy that decouples from it. Here, the entire pediatric titration evidence base is built on specific gravity, and no pediatric source gives a urine osmolality target. This tool therefore ships no osmolality target and will not draw one. Enter an osmolality if you have it: it is plotted, and it is the right test when the specific gravity is untrustworthy. The target band still belongs to specific gravity alone.

    Starting dose: 0.25 to 1.0 mU/kg/hr

    That is the initial infusion rate in the pediatric cohort this tab titrates on: [P] PICU, n=14, acute central diabetes insipidus, 1997. Its own targets were urine output 2–3 mL/kg/hr, specific gravity 1.010–1.020 and serum sodium 140–145 mEq/L. PMID 9467135

    The other three published pediatric starting ranges

    1.3 to 2.7 mU/kg/hr in comatose children, [P] n=3, 1995, the highest published pediatric antidiuretic starting range. PMID 8821902

    0.08 to 0.10 mU/kg/hr during chemotherapy hyperhydration, [P] n=2 treated, 1994, where partial antidiuresis was the aim. PMID 7923015

    0.1 mU/kg/hr in a single infant, [P] n=1, 2021, adjusted first by 50% and then by 25% because the larger step swung the sodium. PMID 33597311

    Four series, three different aims. The tool does not average them, and it names no maximum, because no source states one.

    Time to effect, by source

    Onset is reported at 1 to 6 hours in one pediatric series and from the third hour, peaking at the sixth, in a mixed-age one. These are different studies in different patients and this tool does not average them into a single figure.

    Small patient, small syringe, and the carrier

    At 0.5 mU/kg/hr a 4 kg infant is receiving 2 mU/hr. The only concentration any pediatric report states is 2 units made up in 49.9 mL, 0.04 units/mL, so 40 mU/mL ([P] n=1); at that concentration 2 mU/hr is 0.05 mL/hr. The arithmetic is this page’s; the concentration is the paper’s. PMID 33597311

    Everything that follows is practice, not literature. No source states a carrier rate, a dead-space volume, a minimum pump rate or a concentration rule for this drug in a small child. At tens of microlitres an hour the line between the pump and the patient is the lag on every rate change, a flush or a line change delivers whatever that volume is holding as a bolus, and a dedicated lumen with a carrier running is what keeps the delivered rate close to the set rate. Pick the concentration so the pump runs at a rate it can hold, and treat the first hour after any change as unmeasured.

    Three views on one clock

    Add timepoints to draw the charts. The vasopressin trace is a step plot on purpose: a dose is constant from the moment it is set until it is changed, so joining two dose points with a slope would draw rates the patient never received.

    Where 5 and 10 mU/kg/hr come from, and where they do not

    These are not published limits. No source states a maximum vasopressin dose for central diabetes insipidus at any age, and neither figure appears in any publication as a threshold, a caution point or a ceiling. They are this unit’s figures, and they sit in an empty band: the highest published pediatric antidiuretic starting dose is 2.7 mU/kg/hr (Lee 1995, [P] n=3), the lowest pediatric pressor dose in a randomised trial is 30 mU/kg/hr (Choong 2009, [P] n=65), and there is no pediatric literature between them. PMID 8821902 PMID 19608718

    What each figure is for, and why neither rests on documented injury

    A child above 5 mU/kg/hr is above every published antidiuretic dose, which is a reason to ask whether the diagnosis, the titration or the fluid plan is wrong. A stop at 10 keeps the DI indication cleanly separated from the pressor indication.

    The ceiling rests on dose separation, not on documented injury. No pediatric case of end-organ ischaemia attributable to a vasopressin infusion given for DI is reported. In the pediatric pressor-dose trial the adverse-event rate ratio difference was 12.0% (95% CI −2.6 to 26.7, p = 0.15) and mortality was 10 of 33 against 5 of 32 (RR 1.94, 95% CI 0.75–5.05, p = 0.24) — the authors called that a concerning trend, and neither figure reached significance. Neither is a demonstrated harm, and neither is a licence. PMID 19608718

    Every bar is a published dose range with its population and its n. The two dashed lines are this unit’s figures and carry no citation.

    Fluid alongside the infusion — two published pediatric strategies that disagree

    Restrict, and use an isotonic carrier: normal saline at two thirds of maintenance, sodium held 130–150 mEq/L, [P] n=18. PMID 15211159

    Or replace the excess urine with a hypotonic fluid: 0 to 0.5 normal saline replacing urine output above 3 mL/kg/hr, [P] PICU n=14. PMID 9467135

    The numbers behind each strategy, and what the choice trades

    In 18 children on a perioperative protocol, intravenous fluid was normal saline restricted to two thirds of the estimated maintenance rate plus what was needed for blood loss and haemodynamic stability. Serum sodium was held between 130 and 150 mEq/L, the mean 24-hour sodium change fell from 17.6 ± 9.2 mEq/L in 19 historical controls to 8.36 ± 6.43 mEq/L, and hyponatremia occurred less often on protocol. The paper states no vasopressin dose, so none is attributed to it here. PMID 15211159

    In the 14-child PICU cohort, a low-sodium solution (0 to 0.5 normal saline) replaced urine output above 3 mL/kg/hr and served as maintenance. PMID 9467135

    Neither has been tested against the other. The first concedes some hypernatremia and removes the free-water reservoir; the second keeps the reservoir and depends on the infusion being titrated correctly. Once antidiuresis is established, every millilitre of free water running is retained, which is the mechanism behind the iatrogenic half of phase 2.

    Correction-rate limits, and which of them are pediatric

    Hypernatremia: reduce the sodium by less than 0.5 mmol/L per hour, or less than 10–12 mmol/L per 24 hours. That is the guideline position quoted by the largest pediatric study, 402 episodes in 358 children [P]. The failure direction is cerebral oedema. PMID 36888887

    Hyponatremia: not more than 8 mmol/L in 24 hours in a non-seizing child, target 6–8. That is an institutional pediatric guideline, not pediatric outcome data. The failure direction is osmotic demyelination.

    What each limit rests on, and why the demyelination figures are adult

    The pediatric hypernatremia study found no association between rapid correction and neurological investigation, cerebral oedema, seizures or mortality. It is retrospective and single-centre, so it cannot license faster correction — and its own authors note the 0.5 mmol/L/h standard derives largely from neonatal work. PMID 36888887

    The hyponatremia guideline sets no maximum rate for the seizing child and says explicitly not to delay correction in hyponatremic seizures.

    Everything quantitative about osmotic demyelination is adult. A meta-analysis of 11 cohort studies and 26,710 hospitalised adults put the overall incidence at 0.23%, 0.73% with rapid correction and 0.10% without, odds ratio 3.16 (95% CI 1.54–6.49) — with rapid variably defined across studies as more than 8 to 12 mmol/L in 24 hours, and with some patients developing the syndrome without rapid correction. Pediatric osmotic demyelination appears in the literature only as case reports; no pediatric incidence figure exists. PMID 39967825

    Stopping, interruption, and the transition off

    An interruption shows up late. Polyuria recurred 3 hours after the infusion stopped ([MIX] n=12), and the plasma half-life is 10–20 minutes ([A] n=2). A pump alarm, a line change or a transport presents as rising urine output and falling specific gravity over the next one to three hours, not immediately. PMID 2431834 PMID 30984869

    There is no published wean. No source gives a weaning schedule, a dose to stop at, a recovery threshold or a rate of reduction. The one sourced transition mechanic is an overlap rule: restart the oral agent an hour before the infusion comes off. PMID 33597311

    Two hazards on the way off, and what each cohort was

    The documented signal that endogenous vasopressin has returned is the onset of phase 2 — hyponatremia developing days after the initial DI. Anything more specific than that is local practice, and this tool does not dress practice up as literature. The rapid reversibility is also the stated reason to prefer an infusion over a long-acting agent in an unstable child.

    DI can also appear after the infusion is stopped, in a child who never had it. In an adult-predominant ICU cohort of 1,896 patients given vasopressin for shock, diabetes insipidus after discontinuation occurred in 1.53%, against 2 of 1,320 (0.15%) in a norepinephrine control group; 16 of the 29 cases followed a cardiothoracic intervention including CABG, valve replacement, ECMO and ventricular assist device placement, and no neurosurgical patients were in the cohort. The authors propose transient V2-receptor downregulation as a mechanism — they state it may be involved; they did not demonstrate it. No pediatric equivalent exists. PMID 31567344

    Adipsia is the hidden hazard on transition. In 178 craniopharyngioma microsurgeries (44 children, 134 adults), a hypothalamo-hypophyseal injury score above 3 on postoperative MRI was a potential predictor of adipsic DI, and predicted severe hypernatremia during the admission (OR 15.487, 95% CI 1.852–129.539, p = 0.011) and at follow-up (OR 28.637, 95% CI 3.060–267.981, p = 0.003). A child who cannot feel thirst has lost the safety net that makes an oral regimen work. The cohort pools children and adults, so it is not a pediatric figure. PMID 36120435

    What this tool deliberately does not show

    No desmopressin dose, anywhere. No maximum vasopressin infusion rate, because no source states one at any age. No urine osmolality target on the titration tab, because every pediatric source titrated on specific gravity and none gives an osmolality target. No magnitude for the effect of iodinated contrast on specific gravity: two sources establish the direction, none quantifies the shift or its duration, so the tool states the direction and asks for an osmolality instead. No daily solute-load figure, adult or pediatric. No neonatal threshold for anything, since none is validated; below about 6 weeks the osmolality rules are switched off rather than degraded. No central venous pressure input: it was searched for as a discriminator between SIADH and salt wasting and no study establishes it, and the applicable finding is that the reported measures of volume depletion do not stand scrutiny. No age-banded polyuria thresholds in mL/kg/hr, because none are published; the 2 and 3 mL/kg/hr marks are assembled from studies with different purposes and are labelled as such. And no mean dose from the pediatric brain-death donor series, whose reported standard deviation exceeds its mean.
