00Why this exists

Waveform reading is a trained skill, not an earned one

In 53 tertiary hospitals, 191 clinicians were screened and 109 qualified as competent on the concepts of patient-ventilator asynchrony. Asked then to match asynchrony waveforms to those same concepts, they averaged 3.45 correct out of 8. Formal training in waveform analysis and respiratory mechanics predicted performance: recognition of ineffective triggering was 76.7% in trained versus 59.2% in untrained clinicians, auto-triggering 26.7% versus 12.2%, reverse triggering 30.8% versus 12.2%. Neither years of experience managing invasive ventilation nor professional title predicted anything. Liu 2025, PMID 39905371 adult ICU clinicians

You cannot pattern-match your way to waveform literacy, because the patterns are consequences of one equation. Learn the equation and the patterns become derivable. That is the whole design of this page: a live solver, and text that only ever explains what the solver is doing.

The honest caveat on that study: it measured concept-to-picture matching on a remote platform, not bedside performance, and the sample is adult-ICU clinicians in one country. It is a reason to teach mechanics deliberately, not a measurement of any individual reader.

01The equation

The equation of motion

Pvent + Pmus = VT/CRS + Raw × V̇I + PEEP + PEEPi

Hess writes it with an inertance term and then discards it: the inertance variable, representing the effect of inertia, is assumed to be low and thus disregarded. This tool discards it too, and says so. Hess 2014, PMID 25336536

Pv

Pvent, cmH2O. Pressure the ventilator applies at the airway opening. This is the trace on the top scalar.

Pm

Pmus, cmH2O. Pressure generated by the patient's inspiratory muscles. Positive here means it adds to the ventilator's effort. It is invisible on the airway pressure trace; you infer it from the shape of that trace, which is the single hardest inference in the whole subject.

el

VT/CRS, the elastic term. What it costs to distend the respiratory system by this volume. Depends on volume, not on flow.

res

Raw × V̇I, the resistive term. What it costs to drive this flow through the airways and the tube. Depends on flow, not on volume.

base

PEEP + PEEPi. The baseline the breath starts from: what you set, plus what the patient did not finish exhaling.

The operative sentence. The elastic term is a function of volume and the resistive term is a function of flow, and every waveform finding in critical care follows from separating them. Stop the flow and the resistive term vanishes; what is left is elastic. That is an inspiratory hold. Stop the volume change and the elastic term stops moving; what is left is the baseline. That is an expiratory hold.

Chatburn's taxonomy is the reason to bother learning it as an equation rather than a list: the classification of every mode on every ventilator is built on specifying the means of assisting breaths using control variables specified by the equation of motion, and a mode is fully named by control variable, breath sequence and targeting scheme. Modes proliferate; the equation does not. Chatburn 2014, PMID 25118309 Chatburn 2007, PMID 17328828 Chatburn 2010, PMID 21128755

Two closed forms, both implemented literally in the rig above. The pressure at any instant during a passive volume-control breath is Palv = V/CRS + PEEP. During a passive pressure-control breath it is Palv = ΔP × (1 − e−t/τ) + PEEP. Both are from Hess. PMID 25336536

02Compliance

Compliance

CRS = ΔV/ΔP = VT/(Pplat − PEEP), in mL/cmH2O

It is measured at zero flow, which is why it needs an inspiratory hold. Reference values, all adult adult: Hess 2014, PMID 25336536

  1. Acceptable respiratory-system compliance in mechanically ventilated patients: 50 to 100 mL/cmH2O.
  2. Normal chest-wall compliance: 200 mL/cmH2O. Decreased by morbid obesity, abdominal compartment syndrome, chest-wall edema or burns, thoracic deformity, and by increased muscle tone in a patient fighting the ventilator. Increased by flail chest and by paralysis.
  3. Normal lung compliance: 200 mL/cmH2O. Decreased by ARDS, cardiogenic edema, pneumothorax, consolidation, atelectasis, fibrosis, pneumonectomy, bronchial intubation and over-distention. Increased by emphysema.
  4. They add as reciprocals: 1/CRS = 1/CCW + 1/CL. Hess's worked example, one illustrative patient: CRS 320/(40 − 26) = 23; CCW 320/4 = 80; CL 320/10 = 32 mL/cmH2O. Check: 1/23 is about 1/80 + 1/32. Both are reduced, the lung more so.

The number that axis is measured against

Plateau minus PEEP is driving pressure, and the pediatric guideline puts a limit on it directly. PALICC-2 clinical recommendation 3.3.2 suggests limiting driving pressure to 15 cmH2O, measured under static conditions, in patients with PARDS. It is a conditional recommendation carrying very low certainty of evidence and 82% panel agreement, which is a weaker instrument than the roundness of the number suggests. Emeriaud 2023, PMID 36661420 pediatric, PARDS

Two companion numbers travel with it and are easy to confuse with it. Recommendation 3.3.1 suggests an inspiratory plateau pressure at or below 28 cmH2O in the absence of transpulmonary pressure measurements, with a remark allowing 29 to 32 where chest-wall compliance is reduced; that is an absolute airway pressure and therefore includes PEEP, so it belongs on a different axis from this one and appears on one in section 12. Recommendation 3.2 suggests tidal volumes of 6 to 8 mL/kg, with the explicit remark that volumes below 6 mL/kg should be used where that is what it takes to stay under the plateau and driving-pressure limits. Both are conditional with very low certainty, at 92% and 98% agreement.

Where the pediatric evidence for the driving-pressure limit came from is worth saying plainly. The guideline group reviewing invasive ventilation in PARDS found 26 pediatric studies and had to import 36 adult meta-analyses and systematic reviews to address driving pressure and mechanical power at all. Fernández 2023, PMID 36661436 Of the three numbers quoted above, the driving-pressure limit carries the lowest panel agreement. Ventilate toward it; do not treat it as a measured threshold in children.

The pediatric correction, and it matters

The adult teaching is that when CL and CCW are equal, pleural pressure is half of alveolar pressure. Hess gives Ppl/Palv = CL/(CL + CCW) = CRS/CCW. adult

In 207 mechanically ventilated children with PARDS studied with esophageal manometry over 750 patient-days, the ratio of lung elastance to respiratory-system elastance had a median of 0.83, IQR 0.72 to 0.87. In those children roughly five-sixths of the airway driving pressure appeared across the lung, not half. Respiratory-system compliance tracked lung compliance closely (r = 0.94) and chest-wall compliance only moderately (r = 0.53), and the authors' conclusion was that clinical data cannot reliably substitute for esophageal manometry in separating the two. Ito 2025, PMID 40815194 pediatric, PARDS

So do not import "half goes to the pleural space" into a PICU bedside. The transpulmonary content of a plateau pressure is a measurement, not a constant.

One reason to compute compliance at all, from the study that started the practice: in 15 adults with acute pulmonary failure the end-expiratory pressure producing the greatest oxygen transport and the lowest dead-space fraction, anywhere from 0 to 15 cmH2O, was also the pressure producing the greatest total static compliance. Suter 1975, PMID 234174 adult

Esophageal manometry, transpulmonary pressure and the stress index are the next layer up and are deliberately not on this page. This page's job is to make the airway-opening measurements legible first.

03Resistance

Resistance

RI = (PIP − Pplat)/V̇I    RE = (Pplat − PEEP)/V̇EXH, in cmH2O/L/s

The inspiratory value is only computable in a constant-flow volume-control breath, because you need a known flow at the moment the plateau maneuver interrupts it. Reference values, adult adult, intubated: Hess 2014, PMID 25336536

  1. In intubated, mechanically ventilated patients, RI should be under 10 cmH2O/L/s.
  2. RE is typically greater than RI. This is not a measurement artifact; airways narrow on exhalation.
  3. Hess's worked example: RI = (43 − 40)/0.33 = 9 cmH2O/L/s; RE = (40 − 26)/0.83 = 17 cmH2O/L/s.
  4. Common causes of a rise: bronchospasm, secretions, and a small internal-diameter endotracheal tube.

The third of those is the pediatric one and it deserves its own sentence. Resistance through a tube scales steeply with internal diameter, so the tube can be the dominant resistor in a small child in a way it never is in an adult, and a resistance problem on the waveform may be a tube problem rather than a lung problem. This tool models tube and airway together as one lumped R; it cannot separate them, and neither can the ventilator.

A note on the phrase "high resistance". Marini's caution is that absolute R and C values must be referenced to the size of the aerated compartment. In ARDS, R is high and C is low in absolute units while the specific R and specific C per unit of air-containing lung are much closer to normal. Marini 2011, PMID 21700908 adult The same caution applies with force across the pediatric age range, where absolute compliance in mL/cmH2O spans more than an order of magnitude between a neonate and an adolescent for reasons that have nothing to do with disease.

The tube is part of the resistance, and in a child it is a large part

An in-vitro model of the intubated respiratory system in pressure-control ventilation measured the difference between the peak inspiratory pressure at the Y-piece under flow and the plateau pressure under zero flow, across endotracheal tubes from 3.0 to 8.0 mm, at pressures above PEEP of 10, 15 and 20 cmH2O, at three airway resistances and a range of compliances from normal to extremely severe. Peak pressure overestimated plateau at every tube size. The gap widened with higher compliance, higher resistance, higher set pressure above PEEP and shorter inspiratory time, and inspiratory time was the strongest single variable; the contribution of airway resistance became more prominent as tube size increased. Ilia 2020, PMID 31688716 bench, pediatric-sized tubes

The univariate coefficients as published, for scale rather than for use: compliance β −1.5 (95% CI −1.7 to −1.4) · resistance β 1.7 (1.5 to 2.0) · pressure above PEEP β 1.7 (1.4 to 2.0) · inspiratory time β −0.7 (−1.0 to −0.4), all p < 0.001. That paper also fits multiple-regression models stratified by tube size. Those per-tube models are not implemented here and no predictive equation from that paper is reproduced, because the stratified coefficient tables were not retrieved; the finding is used qualitatively and nothing on this page is back-calculated from it.

The bedside consequence is the one to carry: in pressure control a Y-piece peak is not a plateau, and the smaller the child and the shorter the inspiratory time, the further apart the two are. That is why this rig refuses to print a plateau in any mode until flow has actually stopped, either because you held the breath or because the exponential finished on its own.

Aerosol delivery through the circuit, which is where a resistance problem is often treated, is worked through separately in albuterol and high-flow nasal cannula delivery.

04Time constant

The time constant

τ = R × C

One number that says how fast a lung unit fills and empties. Both directions are exponential with the same τ, but they are not the same exponential. Emptying decays, V(t) = Vi × e−t/τ; filling is its complement, V(t) = V∞ × (1 − e−t/τ), which is the form used for delivered volume in section 06 and the form the solver integrates. A lung unit with a higher resistance or a higher compliance has a longer time constant and needs more time in both directions.

The bedside estimate, and why it is exact here for the wrong reason

τE = exhaled VT / peak expiratory flow

In this tool's model the estimate is not approximate, it is exact. In a passive single-compartment exhalation, flow at the instant expiration begins is (VT/C)/R = VT/τ, so VT divided by that peak flow returns τ identically. That exactness is a property of the assumption, not evidence that the estimate is good. Hess: the method treats the lungs as a single compartment and thus does not account for time constant heterogeneity in the lungs. A real lung is a population of time constants, and the fast units dominate the peak flow while the slow ones dominate the trapping. The method was originally validated on a lung simulator and 10 greyhound dogs. Brunner 1995, PMID 7774225 bench and animal

Why it is worth measuring anyway: the expiratory time constant estimated from the passive expiratory flow curve has been used to derive plateau pressure, compliance and total resistance in real time without an inspiratory pause, in 92 adults across volume-control CMV, volume-control SIMV, volume control plus, and pressure support. That is the only route to those numbers in a spontaneously breathing patient, where a plateau maneuver is invalid. Al-Rawas 2013, PMID 23384402 adult

The rule this whole page returns to. Expiratory time must be long enough relative to τ, and "long enough" means about 3τ for 95% emptying. Everything about auto-PEEP is that sentence.

05Volume control

Volume control: what is held constant

Held constant: tidal volume and inspiratory flow. Free to vary: pressure.

The ventilator delivers a set flow for the time it takes to deliver a set volume. Airway pressure is whatever the equation of motion says it must be:

Paw(t) = PEEP + (V̇set × t)/C + V̇set × R

Read that as two pieces stacked. The V̇set × R piece appears instantly at the start of inspiration and stays flat for the whole breath: that is the vertical step off PEEP. The (V̇set × t)/C piece is the linear climb on top of it. The step is resistance. The slope is compliance. With a constant flow and a linear compliance, the pressure trace is a straight ramp on a pedestal; that shape is why the stress index is defined on a volume-control breath and on no other.

At the end of flow delivery, Paw = PEEP + VT/C + V̇set × R, and this is PIP. Stop the flow with a hold and the resistive pedestal disappears, leaving Pplat = PEEP + VT/C. Hess 2014, PMID 25336536

Flow pattern

Constant, or square, is the default and is the one that makes the arithmetic above true. The decelerating ramp is offered as an option, and the implementation is a modelling decision, not a published waveform: model choice the ramp runs from peak flow down to 50% of peak over TI, so peak flow = VT/(0.75 × TI). Under a decelerating ramp the resistive pedestal shrinks through inspiration and the pressure trace loses its straight-line character. A reader who learned the ramp shape on square flow will misread it.

Limitations

Tidal volume is protected and pressure is not. If compliance falls or resistance rises, the volume is still delivered and the pressure goes wherever it must, which is the safety of the mode and also its hazard. A patient who breathes in against a fixed inspiratory flow gets whatever flow was set and no more; the airway pressure trace dips, breath to breath, and that dip is the classic sign of flow starvation on volume control. Turn on spontaneous effort in the rig, leave the mode on VC, and lower the set flow to watch it appear.

Poor candidate: a high peak pressure with an unexamined plateau. The correct response to a high PIP in volume control is a hold, not a mode change, because PIP alone cannot distinguish a resistance problem from a compliance problem and the two have opposite management. Switching to pressure control to fix a high PIP fixes the number on the screen and nothing else: it converts a pressure problem into a silent volume problem.

06Pressure control

Pressure control: what is held constant

Held constant: applied pressure and inspiratory time. Free to vary: volume and flow.

The ventilator holds Paw = PEEP + ΔP for the whole of TI. Flow and alveolar pressure follow decaying exponentials with the same time constant. All three expressions are Hess's. PMID 25336536

V̇(t) = (ΔP/R) × e−t/τ     Palv(t) = PEEP + ΔP × (1 − e−t/τ)     VT = C × ΔP × (1 − e−TI/τ)

Three consequences the rig lets you discover by moving sliders.

  1. Peak flow is set by ΔP/R, not by any flow control. Raise resistance and the initial flow falls; you have no knob for it.
  2. Whether the set volume arrives depends entirely on TI relative to τ. At TI = 1τ only 63% of the achievable volume is delivered. At 3τ the breath is 95% complete and the residual peak-to-plateau gap is 5% of ΔP; at 5τ it is 99.3% complete and the gap is 0.7%. Neither is zero, so say the number rather than a threshold: the figure below and self-test 14 both use 5τ for "PIP equals Pplat", because 5% of a 20 cmH2O driving pressure is 1 cmH2O, which is a difference a bedside reader would act on. This is the correct way to teach "is your inspiratory time long enough": as a ratio, not as a number of seconds.
  3. When flow reaches zero before TI ends, PIP and Pplat are equal, and the breath has an intrinsic plateau without any hold maneuver. That is a real convenience and a real trap: it is only true when flow actually reaches zero, and in an obstructed patient it never does.

The third of those is not a curiosity in a small patient. In the bench model of the intubated respiratory system across endotracheal tubes from 3.0 to 8.0 mm, peak pressure at the Y-piece overestimated plateau at every tube size, most of all with a short inspiratory time or a high airway resistance. Ilia 2020, PMID 31688716 bench, pediatric-sized tubes Reading a pressure-control peak as though it were a plateau is therefore an error that gets worse as the patient gets smaller.

Limitations

Volume is unprotected. Compliance falls, volume falls, and nothing on the ventilator alarms unless a volume alarm is set. Marini adds the interaction that catches people: because auto-PEEP subtracts from the effective driving pressure in a mode whose set pressure is referenced to the applied PEEP, raising the frequency can be accompanied by a falling tidal volume, so minute ventilation does not rise as much as expected. Conversely, slowing the rate reduces auto-PEEP, restores driving pressure, and can produce an unintended rise in tidal volume. Patients with severe airflow obstruction show greater tidal-volume sensitivity to both inspiratory time and frequency than patients with restrictive disease. Marini 2011, PMID 21700908 adult, obstruction-weighted

Poor candidate: changing compliance. Any process where compliance is moving fast, such as resolving atelectasis, a chest tube going in, a recruitment maneuver, developing edema, or an abdomen being closed, makes pressure control a mode that silently changes the dose. If compliance is the thing that is changing, do not choose the mode that lets volume float.

07PRVC

PRVC, and what actually happens between breaths

Held constant within a breath: pressure. Held constant across breaths: a tidal-volume target. Free to vary: the applied pressure, breath to breath.

PRVC is a pressure-control breath wrapped in a slow outer loop. Every individual breath obeys the pressure-control equations above exactly. After each breath the ventilator compares the measured tidal volume to the target and adjusts ΔP for the next one. The rig implements this control law:

ΔPnext = clamp(ΔP + (VT,target − VT,measured)/Cest, ΔP − 3, ΔP + 3), then clamped to [0, Pmax − PEEP − 5]

model choice The step limit of 3 cmH2O per breath and the 5 cmH2O headroom below the pressure limit are representative of how vendors implement adaptive pressure control. They are not a published constant, and vendors differ from one another in ways that change patient outcomes. The step limit is exposed as a slider in the rig so you can watch an aggressive loop oscillate and a conservative one lag.

Which tidal volume is measured is the entire question

Vendors differ on whether the controller reads inspiratory volume, expiratory volume, volume at the ventilator, or volume at the airway opening, and on whether leak compensation is applied. In a surfactant-deficient, spontaneously breathing juvenile rabbit model (n = 12, 1.61 ± 0.20 kg) run at identical settings with and without an endotracheal-tube leak, the three brands were indistinguishable with no leak. With a moderate leak introduced, the volume-guarantee modes that used proximal volume monitoring and adaptive leak compensation delivered a higher percentage of successfully triggered breaths, higher peak inspiratory pressure, higher minute ventilation, higher pH and lower PaCO2 than the PRVC mode that measured at the ventilator without leak compensation. In that comparison PRVC under-supported the animal. DiBlasi 2019, PMID 30723169 animal, neonatal analogue

The opposite failure, in which the controller reads a falsely low exhaled volume, concludes the lung is stiffening, and escalates pressure to chase gas that is going out around the tube, is the mechanism most often taught. It follows directly from the control law above when the measured volume is expiratory and uncompensated. inference from the control law It is stated here as a mechanistic inference, not as a sourced observation, because the one controlled comparison found the other direction on the ventilator it tested. Both are real; which one a given ventilator does is a property of that ventilator's software.

Poor candidates

  1. An endotracheal tube leak. The target is a measured number and the leak corrupts the measurement. Before trusting PRVC in a child with an audible leak, know where that ventilator measures volume and whether it compensates.
  2. High and variable airway resistance. In four infants aged 1 to 4 months, 4.0 to 4.8 kg, with severe airway obstruction from bronchiolitis, PRVC produced hypoventilation with rising PCO2; switching to volume control at the same set tidal volume improved gas exchange in all four. The proposed mechanism is that in high resistance a pressure-targeted breath leaves gas still in transit when expiration begins, so the set volume does not all reach alveoli, whereas volume control's constant flow drives it in. The authors' conclusion, quoted: "In the mechanical ventilation of a situation of severely increased AR, VCV should be the first choice among modes, as it provides better ventilation of the patient and avoids air-trapping." Four infants with bronchiolitis, not asthma, and a case series: weight it as a mechanism worth knowing, not as a trial result. Medina 2016, PMID 26391736 pediatric, n = 4
  3. A patient with strong, variable effort. The loop reads volume, and a patient's own effort adds volume. A big effort produces a big breath, the controller reads target-exceeded, and support is withdrawn on the next breath, so support falls exactly when the patient is working hardest. inference from the control law

The honest headline finding

In 14 adults with acute lung injury or ARDS studied in a randomized crossover at a 6.4 ± 0.5 mL/kg target, work of breathing trended non-significantly higher on PCV (1.27 ± 0.58 J/L) and PRVC (1.35 ± 0.60 J/L) than on volume control with a high fixed peak flow of 75 L/min (1.09 ± 0.59 J/L), and in 40% of patients tidal volume markedly exceeded the lung-protective target during PRVC and PCV. The authors concluded that PCV and PRVC offer no advantage in reducing work of breathing compared with volume control at a high flow rate, and in some patients did not allow tidal volume to be controlled as precisely. The common belief that a pressure-targeted breath is inherently kinder to a working patient did not survive its own test. Kallet 2005, PMID 16318643 adult ALI/ARDS, n = 14

09Triggering

Triggering

Two mechanisms, one physiology.

Pressure trigger. The exhalation valve holds the circuit at PEEP. An inspiratory effort pulls circuit pressure below PEEP; when the deflection reaches the set sensitivity, the breath is delivered. Typical setting in the comparative literature: −1 cmH2O.

Flow trigger. A bias flow circulates through the circuit. An inspiratory effort diverts some of it into the patient; when the difference between delivered and returned flow reaches the set sensitivity, the breath is delivered. Typical setting in the comparative literature: 0.7 to 2.0 L/min. Tütüncü 1997, PMID 9187592

Before any flow can occur, the effort must first close the gap between alveolar pressure and circuit pressure. That gap is auto-PEEP. The rig models the two thresholds like this, and the teaching point falls out for free: derived

pressure trigger fires when Pmus(t) ≥ auto-PEEP + sensitivityP
flow trigger fires when Pmus(t) ≥ auto-PEEP + sensitivityF × R
  1. Flow triggering is cheaper than pressure triggering only when sensitivityF × R is smaller than sensitivityP. At a flow sensitivity of 1 L/min (0.0167 L/s) and a resistance of 10 cmH2O/L/s, the flow trigger costs 0.17 cmH2O against a 1 cmH2O pressure trigger. At a resistance of 100 cmH2O/L/s, a small tube in a small child, the same setting costs 1.7 cmH2O and the flow trigger has become the more expensive one. Slide R in the rig and watch the two thresholds cross in the readout — but watch it as this model's prediction, not as an observation. derived Two things stand between the crossover and a bedside claim. The rig carries a single lumped R, so the whole of it is charged against the flow trigger, when at a real bedside only the part of the resistance lying between the flow sensor and the alveolus is; section 03 already says the lumping is a simplification, and this is where it bites hardest. And the only human head-to-head in this page's evidence base, Tütüncü 1997, found no significant difference between the two triggers and excluded COPD, which is the population in which a high resistance would drive the crossover. No study confirming the crossover was found.
  2. Neither setting can help with auto-PEEP, because auto-PEEP sits outside the sensitivity term in both expressions. Hess makes this concrete: on an airway-pressure, flow, volume and esophageal-pressure trace in a patient with auto-PEEP, the decrease in esophageal pressure required to trigger the ventilator is the amount of auto-PEEP, expiratory flow does not return to zero, and the inspiratory effort shown fails to trigger at all. The fix is a shorter τ or a longer TE, or applied PEEP to offset the threshold load. Never a more sensitive trigger.
  3. Ineffective efforts are emergent, not scripted. If peak Pmus never exceeds auto-PEEP plus the threshold, no breath is delivered, and the only visible trace of the effort is a notch in the expiratory flow waveform. Hess names exactly this: notching in the expiratory flow waveform suggests missed trigger efforts. The rig gets this behaviour from the model; it is not special-cased anywhere in the code. Hess 2014, PMID 25336536

What the comparative evidence actually shows

In 16 orotracheally intubated adults recovering from acute respiratory failure without COPD, randomized across pressure triggering at −1 cmH2O and flow triggering at 0.7 to 2.0 L/min, each at full and at 75% support, work of breathing during partial support was 0.38 ± 0.24 J/L with pressure triggering and 0.42 ± 0.26 J/L with flow triggering. Tracheal occlusion pressure and rapid shallow breathing index were likewise comparable, and total ventilation, blood gases and haemodynamics did not differ across the four conditions. The authors concluded that the choice of triggering system did not significantly affect short-term gas exchange, mechanics or inspiratory workload. Tütüncü 1997, PMID 9187592 adult, COPD excluded

On a modern ventilator in a patient without severe obstruction, the trigger type is a small effect and the auto-PEEP is a large one. Note the exclusion: that cohort had no COPD, which is precisely the population in which the threshold-load argument bites.

10Breath sequence

Assist-control versus SIMV

Chatburn's taxonomy separates the control variable, which is what the breath holds constant, from the breath sequence, which is which breaths are mandatory and which are spontaneous. They are independent axes, and conflating them is why mode names are confusing: "pressure control" and "assist-control" are answers to different questions. Chatburn 2014 Chatburn 2007 The three phases of every breath, trigger then target then cycle, are the standard framing for reading the interaction. Gilstrap 2013, PMID 24070493 Nilsestuen 2005, PMID 15691392

Assist-control, continuous mandatory ventilation. Every breath is a mandatory breath. If the patient triggers it, it is a mandatory breath. If the timer triggers it, it is the same mandatory breath. The set rate is a floor, not a dose.

SIMV, intermittent mandatory ventilation. A fixed number of mandatory breaths per minute, synchronized to patient effort where one is available; between them, the patient's own breaths, either unsupported or given pressure support. A patient breathing at 30 on SIMV of 12 is doing two different kinds of breath in alternation.

What the comparative outcome evidence shows, and in whom

A systematic review and meta-analysis of eight studies in ventilated neonates compared SIMV against modes that support every breath, meaning assist-control, pressure support and neurally adjusted ventilation. Weaning duration was significantly shorter when all breaths were supported, mean difference −22.67 hours (95% CI −44.33 to −1.01). There was no significant difference in death before discharge (OR 0.74, 95% CI 0.32 to 1.74) or in bronchopulmonary dysplasia at 36 weeks (OR 0.63, 95% CI 0.33 to 1.24), and none in air leaks, extubation failure or any other outcome examined. The authors call for larger studies with explicit ventilator and weaning protocols. Batra 2023, PMID 36631252 neonatal, 8 studies meta-analysed

Say the population plainly: neonates, not older children, and a synthesis of eight small studies rather than a definitive trial. No trial of SIMV against assist-control in the PICU population was found for this page. The direction of the neonatal evidence is that supporting every breath shortens weaning without a measured cost, and the mechanism it points at is the one below.

The one directly relevant piece of pediatric evidence

In six intubated preterm infants being weaned, median postnatal age 34 days, studied on a Draeger Babylog across conventional IMV and patient-triggered ventilation in assist-control at two peak pressures, work of breathing fell from 0.81 J/L on IMV to 0.48 J/L (patient-triggered at 10 cmH2O) and 0.47 J/L (at 15 cmH2O), p < 0.05. Tidal volume rose, 7.5 then 8.2 then 8.5 mL/kg, and the infants dropped their own rate to hold minute ventilation and transcutaneous CO2 constant. Jarreau 1996, PMID 8630564 neonatal, n = 6 For scale, normal work of breathing is 0.3 to 0.7 J/L in adults, and Hess notes it needs an esophageal balloon to quantify properly, is not frequently measured, and it is not clear that measuring it improves outcome. Hess 2014 adult

Six preterm infants on one ventilator is a small and specific study, and the mode names are neonatal. But it is a real measurement in real babies of the exact question, and its direction is that synchronizing every breath costs the patient less work than supporting only some of them.

On SIMV the unsupported breaths are taken through the same endotracheal tube and the same circuit as the supported ones, so the patient pays full resistive cost on every breath the ventilator declines to assist. If you are using SIMV to exercise a patient, be explicit that the exercise is being prescribed in units of the tube.

The asynchrony taxonomy itself, meaning double triggering, reverse triggering, auto-triggering, and early and late cycling by name, is the next layer up and is not covered here. This page's job is to make the trigger, target and cycle phases legible; naming what goes wrong in each is a separate exercise.

11Three controls

Inspiratory time, rise time and cycle-off

Three different controls that the interface makes look similar and the physiology does not.

Inspiratory time

In pressure modes it is set. In volume control it is not settable at all: it is VT/V̇set plus any pause, and the only way to change it is to change the volume or the flow. The rig shows the derived TI and I:E live next to the flow slider. That is the fastest way to make "raise the flow to lengthen expiration" stop being a mantra and start being arithmetic.

Rise time

In this rig it exists only in pressure-targeted breaths. It is how fast the ventilator drives airway pressure from PEEP to PEEP + ΔP. The rig models it as a linear ramp, Ptarget(t) = PEEP + ΔP × min(1, t/trise), settable from 0.05 to 0.4 s. A shorter rise time front-loads flow and pressurizes the circuit faster, which is what a patient with high drive is asking for; a longer one softens the onset.

The direction of effect has been measured on a bench. Across six ICU ventilators at PEEP 5, pressure support of 5 and 10 cmH2O, four triggering sensitivities and inspiratory drives of 300, 500 and 700 mL, a short inspiratory rise time reduced the inspiratory work load, measured as the pressure-time product of airway pressure over the trigger delay, regardless of the support level, the trigger sensitivity or the inspiratory drive. It did not change the part of the delay before the pressure nadir, which is trigger latency and not a rise-time problem. The lowest work load came from a short rise time, a sharp trigger and a high support level together. Murata 2010, PMID 20587100 bench, 6 ventilators

The number on the dial is not portable between machines. That same study records that the rise-time setting was not consistent across the six ventilators and that the measured values differed significantly among them. A separate simulator study of six new-generation ventilators at pressure support 8 and PEEP 5 moved rise time and cycling criteria between their minimum and maximum settings and found significant differences in exhaled tidal volume, inspiratory time and peak flow (p < 0.001) on every ventilator, and states major differences among manufacturers in how both settings behave. Gonzales 2013, PMID 22781498 bench, 6 ventilators So titrate rise time to the waveform in front of you, never to the number, and do not carry a setting across a ventilator change.

An honest limitation. Real ventilators overshoot the target pressure at fast rise settings because of valve and servo dynamics. This linear model has no valve, so it will not show the overshoot spike, and none is drawn. What volume control does not have is a pressure-targeted rise time, because pressure is not the target; this rig gives it the flow waveform shape instead, which is a different control with a different failure mode. It is not true that volume control has no rise-time control at all. Many ICU ventilators expose a flow-onset ramp, or flow acceleration, at the start of a volume-control breath and label that dial rise time — it sets how quickly the set flow is reached, not how quickly a target pressure is reached. A rise-time setting used in volume control at a bedside, such as the one in the practice block of asthma-critical-care.html, is that control and is not a contradiction of this page. No published source establishing the flow-onset behaviour was found, so it is recorded here as an unsourced device observation and no manufacturer is named.

Cycle-off

In pressure control and PRVC, inspiration ends when TI elapses: time-cycled. In pressure support it ends when inspiratory flow decays to a set fraction of that breath's peak flow: flow-cycled. Settable range across ventilators spans roughly 5% to 55% of peak flow, which is the range this rig's cycle-off slider spans and the reason it spans it. That is a statement about devices and it sits in the full text of the cited paper rather than in its indexed abstract; the footer says so. Hoff 2014, PMID 24612764

The relation that makes cycle-off teachable rather than arbitrary: in a passive pressure-support breath, flow is (ΔP/R)e−t/τ, so inspiration ends when e−TI/τ = x, that is: derived from the flow expression in Hess 2014

TI = τ × ln(1/x)

At a 25% cycle-off, TI = 1.39τ. At 5%, TI = 3.00τ. At 40%, TI = 0.92τ. So in a patient with a τ of 1.0 s, dropping the cycle-off from 25% to 5% lengthens every inspiration from 1.4 s to 3.0 s, and takes 1.6 s out of expiration in a patient who by construction needs it most. That is Marini's clinical warning made arithmetic: in severely obstructed patients receiving pressure support, an inappropriately low flow-cycle criterion lets inspiratory flow decelerate slowly, crowds the time available for exhalation, and feeds auto-PEEP that then competes with the applied inspiratory pressure. The fix is to raise the off-switch percentage or set an inspiratory time directly by moving to pressure assist-control. Marini 2011, PMID 21700908

The caveat that must sit under that formula. It holds only when the patient is passive. With active effort the flow trajectory is not a clean exponential and cycling drifts: continued inspiratory effort holds flow up and delays cycling, while expiratory muscle contraction or abrupt inspiratory-muscle relaxation drives flow down and cycles early. Bench work across normal, obstructive, restrictive and mixed lung models at inspiratory efforts of −2, −8 and −15 cmH2O found that changing the flow-cycle level from 5% to 40% did not abolish the late inspiratory rise in airway pressure at medium-to-high effort in most models. Yu 2015, PMID 25371401 bench

And in 16 weaning adults compared prospectively, an automatic breath-by-breath termination criterion selected an effective cycle-off of 31% (23 to 39) against 12% (6 to 23) for a fixed 5% setting, with shorter inspiratory times (1.0 ± 0.3 versus 1.3 ± 0.3 s), smaller tidal volumes (484 ± 88 versus 518 ± 102 mL), higher respiratory rate (24 ± 8 versus 19 ± 6/min), and fewer pressure overshoots and alternative cyclings. The default is not neutral. Hoff 2014, PMID 24612764 adult, n = 16

12Inspiratory hold

The inspiratory hold

What it does. Stops flow at end-inspiration and holds the airway closed for 0.5 to 2 s while pressure equilibrates between the circuit and the alveoli.

What it measures. The elastic term. With flow at zero the R × V̇ term is zero, so the pressure that remains is alveolar. Pplat = PEEP + VT/CRS, and compliance follows as VT/(Pplat − PEEP).

What it requires. Passive inflation. Hess is unambiguous: the measurement is valid only during passive inflation of the lungs, not during active breathing. A plateau read off a patient who is pulling is not a plateau, and the rig flags it as invalid when effort is on. Hess 2014, PMID 25336536

How to read the decomposition. In a real trace the pressure falls in two stages. Immediately on occlusion it drops to the pressure at zero flow, Pz; the gap between PIP and Pz is set by resistance and end-inspiratory flow. Then it continues to fall more slowly to Pplat; that second, smaller gap is set by time-constant heterogeneity within the lung, meaning pendelluft, and by viscoelastic stress relaxation of the tissues.

What the rig gets wrong here, on purpose

A single-compartment linear model has exactly one time constant and no viscoelastance, so this simulator collapses PIP straight to Pplat in a single step with no Pz shoulder. That absence is itself informative: the shoulder you see at a real bedside is direct evidence that the lung is not the thing this model says it is. Run the inspiratory hold above and watch for the step that has no shoulder.

Reference target. Plateau pressure should ideally be kept at or under 30 cmH2O in mechanically ventilated adults, with some evidence supporting under 25 cmH2O in adults with ARDS. adult Both assume normal chest-wall compliance; a high plateau may be safe and necessary when chest-wall compliance is reduced. Hess 2014 The pediatric ARDS guideline limit is 28 cmH2O, again with an allowance for higher values when chest-wall compliance is poor. That is PALICC-2 clinical recommendation 3.3.1, and like the driving-pressure limit beside it, it is a conditional recommendation with very low certainty of evidence, at 92% panel agreement, with a remark permitting 29 to 32 cmH2O where chest-wall compliance is reduced. Emeriaud 2023, PMID 36661420 pediatric Ito 2025 quotes the same limit, and is cited here for the pediatric elastance partition, not as the source of the number. But the study that quantified the pediatric ratio found lung elastance was a median 83% of respiratory-system elastance in children with PARDS, which is the opposite of a large chest-wall allowance. Ito 2025, PMID 40815194 The adult numbers and the pediatric number are printed here with their populations attached and are not blended.

13Expiratory hold

The expiratory hold

What it does. Occludes the airway at end-expiration for 0.5 to 2 s. What it measures. Total PEEP. The pressure at the end of the maneuver in excess of the set PEEP is auto-PEEP. The maneuver was first described, with its haemodynamic consequences, in adults with airflow obstruction. Pepe 1982, PMID 7046541 Blanch 2005, PMID 15636649

Where auto-PEEP comes from, exactly. Incomplete emptying, when expiration is cut off before the lung has finished. Hess gives the closed form, and the rig reproduces it at cyclic steady state to within 0.1 cmH2O:

auto-PEEP = VT / (CRS × (eTE/τ − 1))

Everything clinical follows from reading that expression. Auto-PEEP rises with tidal volume, with resistance and with compliance, both through τ, with rate, and with inspiratory time, the last two because both shorten TE. It falls when you cut minute ventilation, lengthen TE, or reduce resistance with a bronchodilator. Marini's framing sits alongside it: under passive conditions the addressable variables that increase dynamic hyperinflation are increased airway resistance, a long inspiratory duty cycle, and high minute ventilation, and among these the most effective target is usually to reduce ventilation. Marini 2011, PMID 21700908

What it requires. The patient must be relaxed and synchronous; active breathing invalidates the measurement. Marini adds the timing detail: with all airways open, a stable value is reached within 2 to 3 seconds of occlusion.

The rig's expiratory hold runs for 2.0 s for that reason, not because the model needs it. A single-compartment model has no gas left to redistribute once flow stops, so volume is frozen the instant the occlusion begins and the reading is exact at any duration; self-test 20 checks that it is identical at 0.5, 2 and 3 seconds. Take the 2 to 3 s to the bedside and leave the model's indifference here.

What it will lie about, and what the rig cannot show you

The maneuver measures pressure through airways that are still open. When airways close during exhalation, which happens in severe asthma, gas is trapped behind the closure at pressures higher than the occlusion reads, and the measurement underestimates end-expiratory alveolar pressure. Hess illustrates exactly this; Marini describes the resulting signature, a pressure that keeps creeping up for many seconds after occlusion instead of settling by 3 s.

A single-compartment model cannot close an airway, so this simulator will always report a truthful auto-PEEP. That is the model's most dangerous convenience, and it is stated here at the maneuver rather than in a footnote. The asthma-specific consequences, including why a percentage-of-auto-PEEP titration rule collapses when the denominator is falsely low, are not on this page and should not be inferred from it.

The bedside pre-test that costs nothing. Auto-PEEP should be suspected whenever expiratory flow has not returned to zero when the next breath begins. The rig carries a persistent flag in the readout panel that lights whenever end-expiratory flow is non-zero, before any hold is performed.

14The scalars

Reading the three scalars

Pressure-time, flow-time and volume-time, sharing one x-axis, are the whole instrument. Teach each panel as an answer to one question. Hess 2014, PMID 25336536

Pressure-time answers: what did it cost?

In volume control the trace decomposes visually. The vertical step off PEEP is V̇ × R, the slope is V̇/C, the top is PIP, and a hold reveals Pplat. In pressure control the trace is a rectangle by construction and answers nothing about the lung; in pressure modes the information has moved to the flow panel.

Flow-time answers: how fast, and did it finish?

Inspiratory flow above the line, expiratory below. Two facts to extract on every glance: the shape of the expiratory limb, and whether it reaches zero before the next breath starts. The decay is exponential with time constant τ, so a long, low, dragging expiratory limb is a long τ, and τ is R × C.

Volume-time answers: did it all come back?

A difference between inspired and expired tidal volume means a leak: bronchopleural fistula, a leak around the cuff, a leak around a mask. Hess names volume-time graphics as the detector for this, and it is the panel that pediatric practice needs most, because an uncuffed or minimally cuffed tube makes the inspiratory-expiratory gap a routine finding rather than an alarm. This model has no leak, so VTi and VTe match here by construction; the panel is here so the shape is familiar when they do not.

Loops

Offered as an optional toggle in the rig. Pressure-volume shows the enclosed area between inspiratory and expiratory limbs, which widens with resistance. Flow-volume shows the expiratory limb's shape directly, which is where a scooped, concave obstructive pattern reads at a glance. Loops are secondary: on a phone, the three scalars are the tool.

Resistance problem or compliance problem

This is the section the tool is for. Two tables, because the answer inverts between volume control and pressure control, and clinicians routinely carry the volume-control table into a pressure-control patient. Hess 2014, PMID 25336536

Four things that will fool this procedure

i

Unmeasured auto-PEEP. With total PEEP unmeasured, the driving pressure is computed as plateau minus set PEEP, which is falsely high, and respiratory-system compliance is therefore underestimated. A trapping asthmatic reads as a stiff lung. Marini 2011

ii

Pressure control. The peak is set and cannot rise, so a resistance problem never appears as a pressure change at all. It appears as a falling tidal volume and an inspiratory flow that no longer decays to zero. In a small child this compounds with tube size: peak at the Y-piece overestimated plateau at every tube from 3.0 to 8.0 mm, most with a short inspiratory time or a high resistance. Ilia 2020

iii

An active patient. Every number in the procedure requires passive inflation. A patient pulling during inspiration lowers the measured airway pressure and makes the lung look more compliant than it is. The rig refuses to compute compliance from a plateau read during effort, and prints why.

iv

Absolute versus specific mechanics. In ARDS, resistance in absolute units is high and compliance is low, while specific resistance and specific compliance per unit of aerated lung are much closer to normal. Calling the airways narrowed on the strength of a high absolute resistance in ARDS is a real and common inference error. Marini 2011

The one line to carry away. The peak is a flow question and the plateau is a volume question, and you cannot answer either without stopping the flow.

16Limits

What this model cannot show

Bedside monitoring of mechanics during positive-pressure ventilation portrays the lungs as a single compartment and assumes a linear response over the range of tidal volume. Hess calls that a physiologic oversimplification and keeps using it, and so does this tool. The specific things it hides are these. Hess 2014 Marini 2011

  1. Time-constant heterogeneity. One τ, so no pendelluft, no Pz shoulder on an inspiratory hold, and no regional trapping.
  2. Airway closure. No airway in this model can close, so the expiratory hold always reports the truth, and the most important measurement failure in severe asthma is structurally invisible here.
  3. Non-linear compliance. C is a constant, so there is no lower or upper inflection, no recruitment, no tidal over-distention, and the stress index is 1 by construction. Nothing about stress index, transpulmonary pressure or PEEP titration can be learned from this page.
  4. Valve and circuit dynamics. No pressure overshoot at fast rise times, no circuit compliance, no trigger delay beyond what the model's own threshold produces, no imposed work from the demand valve or the tube connector.
  5. A patient. Pmus here is a parameterized waveform with no chemoreceptors. Real respiratory drive responds to what the ventilator does, and unloading the muscles does not reliably reduce neural output.
  6. Gas exchange. No CO2, no oxygen, no dead space. Minute ventilation is displayed; PaCO2 is not, and no version of this tool should ever display it.

The model is worth learning because the four or five places it breaks are exactly the four or five things that make real patients hard, and you can only see a break if you know what unbroken looks like.

18The solver

How the rig computes, and every choice that is not physiology

State variable is V, the lung volume above the relaxation volume at atmospheric pressure. Alveolar pressure is Palv = V/C. Muscle pressure adds to the applied pressure, with Pmus ≥ 0 assisting inspiration. Governing relations, both from Hess and implemented literally: PMID 25336536

V̇ = (Paw + Pmus − Palv)/R    so    dV/dt = (Paw + Pmus − V/C)/R

Over any interval in which the applied pressure is held, the rig uses the analytic solution of that linear equation rather than a numerical integrator:

V∞ = C × (Papplied + Pmus(t + Δt/2))
V(t+Δt) = V∞ + (V(t) − V∞) × e−Δt/τ

This is exact for the model, unconditionally stable, and free of the drift that makes forward Euler fail when τ is short. τ can fall to the tens of milliseconds at the stiff, low-resistance corner of the parameter space, for instance C = 1 mL/cmH2O with R = 10 cmH2O/L/s, which is 10 ms. That corner is a solver stress case, not a small infant on a narrow tube: a narrow tube is the reason resistance is high in a small child, so it lengthens τ rather than shortening it, and the rig's own infant preset (C = 5, R = 100) has a τ of 500 ms, ten times the adult-preset τ per unit compliance. The numerical argument is about the corner, and at that corner forward Euler at Δt = 1 ms already carries several percent per-step error and an explicit Runge-Kutta buys accuracy the exponential update gets for free. Evaluating Pmus at the step midpoint makes the treatment of a time-varying muscle pressure second-order accurate. In volume control the flow is set rather than the pressure, so volume is integrated directly, which is also exact: V(t+Δt) = V(t) + V̇setΔt and Paw = V/C + V̇setR − Pmus.

This page teaches pediatric readers with adult data, and says so at every number. The pediatric mechanical ventilation consensus conference opens with the reason: much of common practice in pediatric mechanical ventilation is based on personal experience and on what pediatric practitioners adopted from adult and neonatal experience, and that is itself named as a barrier to designing and interpreting trials. It issued 152 recommendations from 15 experts, 142 of them (93.4%) with strong agreement, and its abstract carries no numeric ventilator targets, so no threshold on this page is attributed to it. Kneyber 2017, PMID 28936698

The pediatric ARDS guideline group, reviewing invasive ventilation for PARDS from January 2013 to April 2022, found 26 pediatric studies, had to import 36 adult meta-analyses and systematic reviews to address concepts such as driving pressure and mechanical power that remain under-represented in the pediatric literature, and issued exactly one strong recommendation with moderate certainty of evidence across the whole of invasive ventilation, on PEEP. Fernández 2023, PMID 36661436

What verification could and could not confirm

Every identifier on this page — 28 PMIDs — resolves to the first author, journal and year printed beside it. That is the check this page can make and it passed. It is a weaker check than it looks, because a PMID resolving does not confirm that the paper says the thing the sentence attributes to it, and for six specifics on this page the confirming text sits in a full text that was not retrievable during verification rather than in the indexed abstract. They are listed rather than quietly kept:

  • The adult normal ranges and maneuver conventions attributed to Hess 2014 — CRS 50 to 100, CCW and CL 200 mL/cmH2O, inspiratory resistance under 10 cmH2O/L/s, work of breathing 0.3 to 0.7 J/L, hold duration 0.5 to 2 s, 100 Hz waveform digitization, and the adult plateau targets of 30 and 25 cmH2O. That paper is a single-author narrative review and was not openable during verification. These are conventional textbook ranges rather than measured thresholds, and nothing in the solver depends on any of them: they are printed for scale beside computed numbers and are never used as a coefficient. Treat them as convention with a citation attached.
  • The IQR 0.72 to 0.87 for EL/ERS (Ito 2025). The median of 0.83, the n = 207, the 750 patient-days and the r = 0.94 and r = 0.53 correlations are in the abstract and were confirmed; the interquartile range is not.
  • The four infants aged 1 to 4 months at 4.0 to 4.8 kg, and their authors' quoted conclusion (Medina 2016). PubMed indexes no abstract at all for that record, which the source table above also states.
  • The per-mode work-of-breathing values 1.27, 1.35 and 1.09 J/L and the 6.4 ± 0.5 mL/kg target (Kallet 2005). The n = 14 and the 40% of patients whose tidal volume exceeded the lung-protective target are in the abstract and were confirmed; the per-mode numbers are not.
  • The 2 to 3 s an expiratory occlusion needs to settle (Marini 2011), which is the reason the rig holds its expiratory occlusion for 2.0 s.
  • The 5% to 55% cycle-off range across ventilators (Hoff 2014), which is the reason the cycle-off slider spans that range. The 31% against 12% comparison from the same paper is in the abstract and was confirmed.

None of the six is load-bearing for the simulator. The solver runs on the equation of motion and on the reader's own slider values, and no number in that list is a coefficient, a threshold the code tests against, or an input to any figure; they are reference values printed beside computed ones. A reader who needs to act on one should read the source rather than this page.

No number on this page was derived, back-calculated, or reconstructed from a published figure, and no published figure is reproduced, traced or embedded. Where a value is a modelling decision rather than a finding, it is labelled model choice where it appears and again in the solver table above. Where a relation was derived here in closed form from a published expression rather than quoted, it is labelled derived. Three specific gaps are recorded rather than papered over: no source found reports representative compliance and resistance values for mechanically ventilated children by age or diagnosis, so every pediatric preset is author-chosen; no vendor publishes an adaptive-pressure control law, so the PRVC loop is author-chosen and attributed to no manufacturer; and no controlled human study of PRVC pressure escalation with an endotracheal-tube leak was found, so that mechanism is labelled an inference from the control law rather than a sourced observation.
