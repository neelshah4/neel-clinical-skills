Five things that are just true

Levy noticed the problem in 1979. Rothe explained what the container was actually doing. Brengelmann closed it with conservation of energy. What follows is that whole argument built from five plain facts, with three working rigs and seven PICU cases at the end.

Everything that follows is an argument about which of these five facts causes which. Nobody disputes the facts themselves. If you hold all five in your head at once, most of the seventy-year fight dissolves on its own.

01

The loop is closed, and the blood volume in it is fixed. Over the next thirty seconds, no blood is created or destroyed. Whatever piles up in one place came out of another place.

02

Blood vessels are stretchy bags, not rigid pipes. A stretchy bag holds more when the pressure inside it is higher. Veins are floppy and hold a lot. Arteries are stiff and hold a little.

03

There is one engine. The heart is the only thing continuously adding energy to the blood. (Breathing muscles and leg muscles add some, and that matters later.)

04

Pushing fluid through a narrow place costs pressure. The size of the drop is flow times resistance. The drop is the price of the flow, not the reason for it.

05

In a steady state, what leaves the heart per minute enters it per minute. Cardiac output and venous return are one number measured in two places.

The one analogy to keep

You are riding a bicycle at 25 miles an hour and there is wind in your face. Two ways to describe this:

"There is wind in my face, therefore I am going fast."
"I am going fast, therefore there is wind in my face."

The second one is right. The wind is the price of the speed. Your legs are the engine. If you doubled the wind by riding into a gale, you would not speed up.

Every pressure difference in the circulation is that wind. It is real, it is measurable, it is proportional to the flow, and it is not the engine. Hold that thought through part II, because it is the entire argument.

ARig one

Two tanks and a pump

Before any physiology vocabulary, build the whole circulation out of two buckets. A narrow stiff bucket (the arteries) and a wide floppy bucket (the veins), joined by a narrow pipe (the resistance vessels). A pump lifts water from the floppy bucket into the stiff one. Water leaks back through the narrow pipe.

The total amount of water never changes. That is fact 01, and it is the only trick in the entire argument.

What the rig shows. You never touched the floppy bucket. Its level fell because the pump ran faster and moved water into the stiff one. Set the pump to zero and both levels meet at one number: that is mean systemic pressure. This lumps all the veins into one tank, so the venous reading sits above a real central venous pressure; rig 3 separates them. One caveat the model hides. In a living animal the convergence is not this clean. Recording zero-flow pressures for ten minutes in 14 pigs, van Loon and colleagues found the equilibrium depended on how the arrest was produced and on residual reflexes: pentobarbital equilibrated quickly (mean arterial pressure 47 ± 3.7 to 16 ± 2.5 mmHg), while ventricular fibrillation left a persistent retrograde gradient of 5 ± 2.1 mmHg with central venous pressure above arterial. Their conclusion is that mean circulatory filling pressure is not a stable, universal value (ref 27).

Two things worth doing before moving on. Set the pump to zero and watch both levels converge; the level they meet at is the pressure the whole system settles to when flow stops. Then hold the pump steady and pull water out; every level falls together. Those are the only two ways to change that zero-flow number, and they will come back as Rothe's central point.

IGuyton, 1955

A new gradient

Guyton faced a genuinely hard problem. The circulation is a closed loop, so every measurement contaminates every other one. Speed the heart up and the vessels change; change the vessels and the heart's filling changes. There is no clean place to stand.

His solution was to cut the loop. In the laboratory he took the heart out of the circuit and replaced it with a mechanical pump. Now the vascular system could be characterised on its own: set a flow, read the pressure at the right atrium, repeat.

The result was a straight line sloping down. At high flow, right atrial pressure was low. At low flow it was higher. At zero flow everything equilibrated at one number, which he called the mean circulatory filling pressure. That number was not new; the insight was that it told you something real about the vessels themselves, independent of the heart.

VR = (MCFP − RAP) / ZVR

Then came the move that made him famous. He put that line on the same axes as Starling's cardiac function curve. Where they crossed was the only state that both the heart and the vessels would accept. Cardiac output and right atrial pressure were not chosen by either half. They fell out of the intersection.

What was genuinely brilliant

Neither the heart nor the circulation independently sets cardiac output. That is true, it was a real advance, and it is still the reason the diagram is taught. Change the volume and the vascular line shifts; change contractility and the cardiac curve shifts; the new crossing point tells you where the patient will land.

Where it went wrong, and it went wrong in the same paper

The mathematics described a relationship. The words described a mechanism. Guyton wrote that right atrial pressure was not a primary determinant of cardiac output, that it was settled simultaneously with output by the interaction of heart and vessels. He also wrote, a few pages away, that right atrial pressure was a back pressure opposing venous return, and that mean circulatory filling pressure promoted it.

It is quite obvious that the greater the right atrial pressure, the greater is the back pressure in the veins preventing the return of blood to the heart.

Guyton, Physiological Reviews 1955

That second sentence is what stuck, because it is easy. A pressure difference, a resistance, a flow. It sounds like a garden hose. And a graph naturally reads as a causal story: something on the horizontal axis, something rising on the vertical, a line between them. But an axis is not an arrow. The direction of causation was never in the plot.

IILevy, 1979

The numerator strikes back

Levy did not attack the equation. He rewrote it, which is worse.

VR = (Pms − RAP) / RVR    becomes    Pms − RAP = VR × RVR

Identical algebra, opposite story. On the left it looks like a gradient producing a flow. On the right it looks like a flow producing a gradient. Nothing in the mathematics chooses between them.

But the experiment does choose. In Guyton's preparation, the investigator turned a knob on a mechanical pump and then measured the pressure at the right atrium. Flow was set. Pressure was read. That is the definition of an independent and a dependent variable, and the venous return curve had them backwards.

So where do the pressures come from?

Levy's answer is fact 01 and fact 02 together. The heart moves blood from the venous side to the arterial side. As flow rises, arterial pressure rises, and because arteries are stretchy, the arterial compartment now holds more blood. Total volume is fixed. That extra arterial blood had to come out of the veins. Venous pressure falls, and right atrial pressure with it.

Right atrial pressure did not limit anything. It fell because the pump moved blood uphill and there was less left to distend the veins.

The disappearing gradient

Then Levy pointed at the numbers. Take mean systemic pressure at about 7 mmHg and right atrial pressure at about 2 mmHg. The famous gradient for venous return is 5 mmHg. Now look at what the heart actually generated: 100 mmHg at the aorta against 2 at the right atrium, a difference roughly twenty times larger.

Read those as the classical animal-preparation figures, not as patient values. The 5 mmHg gradient holds up: Magder puts the difference between mean systemic filling pressure and right atrial pressure at 3 to 6 mmHg (ref 15). A Pms of 7 mmHg does not hold up; human bedside estimates run roughly 19 to 21 mmHg, as part III sets out. The comparison itself is unaffected, since 98 over 5 is about twenty either way. Levy's full text could not be retrieved this run to confirm that these are the numbers he used, so treat them as the figures the argument is conventionally told with rather than as quoted values.

If the 5 mmHg gradient were really the engine, then raising right atrial pressure by 5 mmHg would stop the circulation entirely. It does not. Patients live at a central venous pressure of 15. The small number was never the energy source; it was a pressure difference created inside the circulation as blood passed through it.

The reply this page cannot source

The story usually told next is that the editors sent Levy's paper to Guyton, that Guyton's response ran alongside it, and that he conceded almost every conceptual point. No such reply is indexed. A PubMed author search returns nine Guyton items for 1979, none of them in Circulation Research and none about venous return, and Brengelmann's 2019 review lists no Guyton comment accompanying Levy 1979. The one 1979 Circulation Research defence of the concept that does exist is by someone else: Alexander, "In defense of 'venous return'" (ref 11). Levy's own full text returned HTTP 403 and has no open-access copy, so an appended editorial note can be neither confirmed nor ruled out. Until somebody produces it, this page does not rely on it.

What Guyton did put in print, and what the argument here rests on instead, is in the 1955 review: right atrial pressure is not one of the primary determinants of cardiac output but is itself determined simultaneously along with cardiac output (ref 2). That is the concession the story wants, in his own words, twenty-four years earlier.

The point carried by the story is worth making on its own terms. Levy's preparation made flow independent because an investigator was holding the knob. The intact heart holds no such knob. It cannot select an output and impose it. The variables that really are upstream are contractility, heart rate, vascular resistance, vascular capacitance, and blood volume; output, venous return, arterial pressure and venous pressure are all downstream of those, resolved together. Attribute that to the physiology, not to a letter nobody can produce.

The actual state of play after 1979. Both men were doing legitimate "what if" analyses. Guyton temporarily froze right atrial pressure as independent to draw one curve; Levy temporarily froze flow as independent to draw the other. Neither freezing describes the intact circulation. Pressure does not determine flow alone. Flow does not determine pressure alone. The system resolves them together.

IIIRothe, 1983 and 1993

Return of the elastic state

If pressure and flow are both outputs, what are they outputs of? Rothe's answer: the shape of the container.

Three words people use interchangeably and should not

The vocabulary that makes the rest of it possible.
TermWhat it actually means
CapacityHow much blood is in that compartment right now. A number, in mL.
ComplianceThe slope, C = ΔV / ΔP. How much extra volume it takes to raise the pressure by one unit.
CapacitanceThe whole volume-pressure relationship, including where it starts. Two vessels can share a slope and still be wildly different sizes.

Rothe's linear description of a vascular segment:

V = Vu + C × P

Vu is the unstressed or rest volume: how much the container holds when the distending pressure is zero. C times P is the extra volume associated with actually stretching the wall.

The flat firehose

Take an empty firehose and start filling it. At first the cross section just goes from squashed oval to round. The circumference never changes, so the wall never stretches, so the pressure barely rises. That filling is the rest volume, and it is a lot: roughly three quarters of the blood in the body sits in vessels at or near this state. Berger and Takala put the stressed fraction at around 25% of total blood volume (ref 13), and Brengelmann's model, the one rig 3 reimplements, is built on the same assumed quarter (ref 6).

Only once the hose is round does adding more force the circumference open, stretch the wall, and drive the pressure up. That extra is what people call stressed volume.

The correction almost everyone needs. Stressed and unstressed volume are not two pools of blood sitting in separate compartments. Inside a pressurised vessel, every millilitre is at the same pressure and every millilitre is equally under stress. The split is a bookkeeping line drawn on one contained volume, a way of separating the resting size of the container from the extra associated with stretching it. There is no hidden reserve tank waiting to be tapped.

What mean systemic pressure actually is

Pms = ΣVs / ΣC

Total stressed volume divided by total compliance. Not the pressure of any one vessel, and not the average of the pressures you measure during flow. It is a property of the whole elastic container: how full it is relative to how big it is.

Which gives exactly two ways to raise it. Put more blood in. Or make the container smaller.

What the number measures out at, which is not the textbook figure

Animal zero-flow measurements sit below 10 mmHg. Human bedside estimates do not. In mechanically ventilated post-cardiac-surgery patients, Pmsf by inspiratory hold was 18.8 ± 4.5 mmHg at baseline and rose to 29.1 ± 5.2 after 500 mL of colloid (ref 23). A later series in the same population gave Pmsf 20.9 ± 5.6, stop-flow arm pressure 19.8 ± 5.7 and the model analogue 14.9 ± 4.0 mmHg (ref 24).Erratum on record for PMID 22584797 (ref 24) — Intensive Care Med 2013;39(1):163. Linkage only, not retrieved; consult it before relying on these three figures. In healthy awake preoperative patients the stop-flow arm pressure was 22 ± 5 mmHg (ref 25). Reviewing the field, Berger and Takala put living critically ill patients at 18 to 33 mmHg against animal values below 10 (ref 13).

Magder's 2025 review sets a target rather than reporting a measurement: mean systemic filling pressure should ideally sit under 10 mmHg to limit capillary drainage, with the gradient from it to right atrial pressure generally 3 to 6 mmHg (ref 15). That last point deserves more room than the argument on this page gives it. The same number read as the upstream pressure for venous return is also the downstream pressure for capillary filtration, so a high one carries an edema cost whatever you believe about causation.

The classical "7 to 10 mmHg" range could not be traced to Rothe this run. His 1993 abstract reports no numeric value and the full text returned HTTP 403. The animal half of that figure is supported only indirectly, through ref 13; the human half is contradicted by refs 23 to 25.

Passive versus active, and why it matters clinically

Passive redistribution

The heart works harder, blood shifts from veins to arteries, venous volume and pressure fall. The veins recoil onto a lower point of their existing curve. Their properties never changed. No venoconstriction happened. Pms is unchanged.

Active venoconstriction

Venous smooth muscle contracts and the container itself changes, usually by reducing unstressed volume and sometimes by changing compliance too. The same blood now sits at a higher pressure. Pms rises with no change in blood volume.

Rothe's warning is that these look similar in an experiment and are frequently confused. A vascular bed that loses inflow will passively empty and can be mistaken for one that actively constricted.

The pivot, and the step too far

During flow, pressure falls all the way from aorta to right atrium. Arterial pressure is above Pms and right atrial pressure is below it, so somewhere along the way the local pressure must cross the numerical value of Pms. Stop the heart and the compartments above that point lose volume while the ones below it gain; near the crossover, almost nothing moves. The name usually attached to it is the pivot pressure, and it is usually attributed to Rothe. His 1993 abstract does not use the word, and Brengelmann's 2019 paper does not discuss a pivot at all; the phrasing turns up in Berger and Takala's 2018 review, in their own voice and unattributed (ref 13). As a descriptive marker it is fine, and because most volume and compliance live in the small veins and venules, that is usually where it falls.

Then he went further. Because the pressure in the small veins during flow happens to be close to the number Pms, he treated Pms as an estimate of their distending pressure, and therefore as the upstream pressure driving venous return. His 1993 abstract says exactly that.

That is a coincidence of magnitude being promoted to a physical identity. An equilibrium property of the entire container was given an anatomical address it does not have. Which is where the next rig comes in.

CRig three

The pressure staircase

This is the picture that settles it. The circulation is drawn as thirteen segments in series, from aorta to vena cava, using Rothe's tabulated volumes, pressures and resistances. Each step is one segment. The width of a step is its compliance. The height is its distending pressure. So the area of a step is its distending volume, and the total shaded area is the total distending volume in the body.

That total is pinned. It cannot change when you move flow, because blood is neither created nor destroyed. Everything else follows.

What the model gives, with vascular properties held constant, is a crossing that does not move. It sits at 31.4% of cumulative compliance at every flow from 1 to 8 L/min. That is algebra rather than a bug: the crossing occurs where accumulated resistance equals the ratio of two constants, and no flow term survives. Let arteriolar resistance adjust to defend aortic pressure and the pivot does travel, from 28.2% at 3 L/min to 32.8% at 7 L/min. So a pivot that moves with flow needs a circulation with working reflexes and does not appear in one with frozen properties. The deeper point is untouched either way: a crossing point is not a compartment held at Pms, and its stability under fixed conditions is precisely why the coincidence was mistaken for an anatomical fact.

What it does not model. The segments here stay linearly compliant at all pressures, so nothing collapses. Real venous return curves flatten at strongly negative right atrial pressure because the great veins partially collapse and further falls in pressure stop changing anything upstream. That plateau is absent from this rig by construction. Treat the negative terminal pressures as the model telling you the terminal segment has run out of volume, not as a pressure a vein would actually hold.

IVBrengelmann, 2003

The phantom pressure

Rothe had given Pms an address in the small veins. Brengelmann went back to the apparatus to ask whether anything actually lived there.

What the machine actually did

Guyton drained the right atrium through an external collapsible tube, a Starling resistor, into a mechanical pump. The pump delivered into the pulmonary artery and the left heart returned the same flow to the systemic circulation. An external reservoir was connected but closed while each curve was recorded, so the volume inside the peripheral vasculature stayed fixed. There was one flowmeter, on the pump outflow, and every point was taken after a new steady state had settled.

Read that last sentence again. At steady state, pump output and cardiac output and venous return are necessarily the same number. So the experiment never measured venous return as a flow distinct from cardiac output. It measured the steady flow through the vascular subsystem and the right atrial pressure that came with it.

Why the curve looks causal, and why that is a drawing convention

Here is the cleanest thing in the 2003 paper. When you graph negative feedback between two subsystems, one of the two input-output relationships has to be plotted backwards, with its input variable on the vertical axis. In Guyton's diagram, the venous return curve is the one drawn backwards. It has to be, or the two curves could not share a set of axes.

So the visual impression that right atrial pressure is doing something to flow is an artefact of the plotting convention that makes the intersection possible in the first place. Brengelmann's specific target is the claim this encourages: that raising right atrial pressure to the level of mean systemic pressure would stop venous return.

You cannot move right atrial pressure by itself

Raise the Starling resistor and right atrial pressure has to settle higher. But the right atrium and the nearby great veins must then contain more blood to hold that higher pressure, and total volume is fixed, so that blood comes from somewhere upstream. The whole distribution shifts and flow cannot stay where it was. Whether Guyton reset the pump by hand or the collapsing tube throttled its filling turns out not to matter. Either way the recorded point is just the flow and pressure jointly compatible with the resistor height, the vascular properties and the fixed blood volume.

The ledger experiment

Then Brengelmann proposed a different apparatus that produces the same curve. Take out the Starling resistor. Let blood from the right atrium spill through an open tube into an external reservoir, with the tube height setting right atrial pressure. A pump draws from the reservoir and returns blood to the pulmonary artery. The reservoir is not an energy source. It is a visible ledger of how much blood has entered or left the peripheral vasculature.

  1. Stop the pump. Set the tube so right atrial pressure equals Pms. Everything equilibrates and the vasculature holds a reference volume, call it V0.
  2. Start the pump at some flow with the tube unmoved. Upstream pressures rise above Pms and those compliant compartments expand, so the vasculature now holds more than V0. The extra came out of the reservoir.
  3. Lower the tube. Right atrial pressure falls, outflow briefly exceeds inflow, and blood drains back into the reservoir until the vasculature holds exactly V0 again.
  4. You now have one point: a flow, and the right atrial pressure that goes with it at volume V0. Repeat at a new flow. The order can be reversed with no difference.

Trace the points and you get Guyton's curve. Nothing in the apparatus was ever held at Pms. All it took was paired changes in flow and right atrial pressure, real segmental pressure differences, compliant compartments, conservation of blood volume, and the discipline of returning to the same vascular volume at every point.

The equation survives. The picture does not.

Brengelmann then ran a three-compartment model with an ideal pump. At zero flow all three compartments sat at Pms. The moment flow started, none of them did: arterial pressure rose above it, both venous pressures fell below it, and volume moved accordingly. The model still produced the familiar near-linear flow against right atrial pressure relationship, and its algebra still rearranged into flow equals (Pms minus right atrial pressure) over an effective resistance.

Pms is in that derivation for one reason: total vascular volume was stipulated to be fixed, and at zero flow that volume sits across the combined compliances at one common pressure. Pms is the compact way of writing that constraint. The derivation never found a vessel held at Pms; it introduced the zero-flow pressure while doing volume accounting under flow.

The denominator misleads in the same way. Once Pms was placed in the small veins, the resistance to venous return invited reading as a physical venous resistance sitting between it and the right atrium. It is not. It is a composite of arterial and venous resistances together with how compliance is distributed across the network, which is exactly why Guyton first called it an impedance.

So what is the phantom

Local pressure gradients are entirely real. Neighbouring segments hold different pressures at the same instant, blood passes through real resistive pathways, and pressure falls along them as energy is dissipated. None of that is in question.

The gradient from Pms to right atrial pressure has a different status. Right atrial pressure is a local pressure in a flowing circulation. Pms is the common pressure reached when flow stops. The equation sets them side by side as though they were opposite ends of one physical pathway. This is not water running downhill, where the high and low points exist at the same moment and gravity releases the stored energy. In the circulation the heart supplies the energy and sets up the whole flowing profile.

The chain, and where it breaks. A local venular pressure numerically approximates Pms. That pressure is therefore called Pms. Pms is then assumed to persist there during flow. It therefore supplies an upstream driving pressure opposed by right atrial pressure across a resistance to venous return. Only the first step follows from the data.

The pivot keeps its descriptive value. It marks the crossover between regions that gain and lose volume as the circulation changes state, and it sits among the small veins because that is where the volume and the compliance are. But a crossover that moves is not an equilibrium pressure with an address. The pivot is real as a crossing point. The pressure is real as a local pressure. The phantom is the identity assigned to it.

One loose end, deferred to the next section. Brengelmann's pump imposed flow, so the model shows how a fixed volume redistributes at different flow rates without asking how much flow a real heart could sustain at a given vascular state. If Pms is not a pressure source, why does the elastic state of the circulation so obviously constrain achievable output? That is where the other side of the argument makes its case, and the current statement of it is Magder's 2025 review (ref 15), not the 2006 Point.

VBrengelmann, 2019

The energy argument, which is the one that actually closes it

The last section ends with this objection previewed and deferred, so here it is. Everything above is about interpretation. This one is about physics, and Brengelmann made it central sixteen years after the paper the last section was built on.

Picture the standard diagram: an elastic bag at Pms, draining through a resistance against a back pressure at the right atrium. Ask where the energy for that outflow comes from. The bag is elastic, so it stores energy in its stretched walls. But to release that energy it would have to shrink, and it is not shrinking. It is being refilled at exactly the rate it drains, at constant volume and constant pressure, forever.

A container held at fixed volume and fixed pressure cannot supply the work of driving its own outflow. It is a passive element in the pathway. Whatever drives its inflow is the engine. That is the heart, and only the heart.

The symmetry that gives the game away

Guyton's expression comes from one algebraic step: writing every compartment pressure as right atrial pressure plus the intervening pressure drops, then imposing the constraint that total volume is constant. Do that and you get (Pms − Pra) / F on one side and a ratio of resistance-times-compliance terms on the other, which has the units of resistance. Call it the resistance to venous return.

Now write the same compartment pressures as arterial pressure minus the intervening drops. Same constraint, same algebra, and you get:

F = (Pa − Pms) / RAD

Equally valid. Equally derivable. Had Guyton plotted flow against arterial pressure instead, we would have spent seventy years teaching that the way to raise cardiac output is to lower Pms, the back pressure opposing arterial pressure. Nobody says that, because it sounds absurd. It is exactly as absurd as the version we do teach.

The appearance of Pms in either expression has nothing to do with what drives flow. It is there because you stipulated that total volume is constant, and Pms is just total distending volume divided by total compliance. It is bookkeeping.

The fair caveats, because this is not one-sided

  1. The diagram still predicts well. Bleed a patient and the vascular curve shifts left at unchanged slope; the new intersection is lower output at lower filling pressure. That is right. The objection is to the mechanism, not the forecast.
  2. Acutely dropping right atrial pressure does transiently raise venous return. Brengelmann grants this explicitly. That is exactly why we keep a separate word for venous return. What he denies is that the imbalance persists into the new steady state.
  3. Muscle pumps are real engines. Respiratory and skeletal muscle pumping genuinely add energy. The criticism is aimed narrowly at steady-state elastic recoil at fixed volume.
  4. The argument is live, and the other side has the more recent papers. Magder's 2006 Point holds that the classical Guyton view is correct, and his 2025 review restates it (ref 15). Two different experimental preparations get run together here and should not be. Møller and colleagues used a closed-chest, centrally cannulated porcine heart-bypass model on VA ECMO and concluded that right atrial pressure acts as back pressure to venous return (ref 18); Berger and colleagues used right atrial balloon occlusion in pigs with an intact circulation, which is a different design answering a different question (ref 19). Beard and Feigl argue the concept should be removed from teaching outright. Sunagawa argues it should be taught, conceding the causal point and denying that it matters, since at constant stressed volume venous return and venous pressure hold a fixed reciprocal relationship (ref 12). This is not settled.
VIBedside

Seven PICU cases, worked

Each one has a reflex answer that most of us were taught, what is physically happening instead, and what actually changes about the plan. Open them one at a time.

01Six-month-old in septic shock, 20 mL/kg over 15 minutesCVP goes 4 to 9. What did the bolus do?+
The reflex answer

The bolus raised mean systemic pressure, which widened the gradient for venous return, so more blood came back and output rose.

What is actually happening

You added volume to a container of fixed size, so the total distending volume rose. Nearly all the compliance is on the venous side, so nearly all of the new volume landed there and raised venous distending pressure, including in the segments that fill the right ventricle. Right ventricular end-diastolic volume rose.

Whether output rises now depends on one thing only: where that ventricle sits on its own Starling curve. The heart is what converts filling into flow. The bolus did not push blood forward. It made more blood available for a ventricle that may or may not be able to use it.

What changes about the plan

Measure the output, not the filling pressure. If CVP rises and stroke volume does not, you did not buy flow, you bought congestion, and you should stop. Then look downstream for the cost: portal vein pulsatility, hepatic vein and renal venous Doppler patterns, urine output over the next several hours. A rising CVP with a flat stroke volume is the clearest signal in the room that the next bolus is a bad idea.

Read the Doppler and the filling pressure as complementary rather than interchangeable. A 2026 review makes the distinction cleanly: CVP is a continuous readout of the risk of congestion, while VExUS is an intermittent organ-level look at congestion that has already happened, and neither substitutes for the other (ref 31).

02Pediatric ARDS, PEEP up from 8 to 14, CVP 8 to 14, cardiac index fallsThe number went up and the flow went down. Why?+
The reflex answer

PEEP raised right atrial pressure, which opposes mean systemic pressure, so the gradient narrowed and venous return fell.

What is actually happening

Three separate things, and none of them is a back pressure.

First, juxtacardiac pressure rose. The measured CVP went up while the transmural pressure that actually distends the right ventricle went down. The number rose and the filling fell, at the same time, for the same reason.

Second, the intrathoracic great veins are among the most compliant and lowest-pressure segments on the staircase. Squeezing them from outside shrinks the container exactly where the margin is thinnest, and pushes volume out of the chest into the abdomen and periphery.

Third, if any lung is now overdistended, pulmonary vascular resistance rises and right ventricular afterload rises with it. That is a separate mechanism and it is often the dominant one.

What changes about the plan

Stop reading absolute CVP under high PEEP. Read the trend, and interpret it against pleural pressure. Ask whether the PEEP step is recruiting or overdistending by looking at driving pressure and compliance at each step; if compliance stopped improving and output fell, the PEEP bought nothing and cost flow.

This case also explains why bedside estimation of Pms from inspiratory hold maneuvers is on shakier ground than it looks. Brengelmann's own caution is that the maneuver may change segmental rest volumes and compliances on both sides of the diaphragm, which changes Pms by definition, and may recruit reflex changes in segmental resistance. His simulation shows that adjusting arteriolar resistance alone shifts both the extrapolated Pms and the apparent slope, with no change in any other resistance in the model.

That is no longer only a simulation. Measured in pigs against right atrial balloon occlusion, the inspiratory-hold estimate overshot true mean systemic filling pressure by 3.0 (SD 5.1) mmHg, and raising PEEP from 5 to 10 raised the true value from 12.9 to 14.0 mmHg with no change in flow (ref 19). A later study in the same laboratory found all three indirect estimates ran low against the zero-flow measurement, with volume status changing the size of the error (ref 20).

03VA ECMO circuit chatter at target flowThe single best demonstration in the whole argument+
The reflex answer

The patient is hypovolemic. Give volume.

What is actually happening

On VA ECMO you are Guyton's preparation. You are literally holding the pump knob, and right atrial pressure is the reading. So the causal direction is not in dispute here, it is in your hand.

Raise the flow and volume redistributes uphill toward the arterial side, exactly as in rig 3. The last step on the staircase, the cavae at your drainage cannula, has the lowest distending pressure and the smallest margin. It empties first. When its distending pressure reaches zero the vessel starts to change shape, the geometry interferes with flow, and you get chatter.

The model puts numbers on it. In the reference state the central venous compartment holds 233 mL of distending volume. Vasodilate the venous bed, which is what sepsis and most of your sedation does, and that falls to 37 mL. Now push the flow back up to restore an aortic pressure of 136, and it falls to 10 mL with a computed terminal pressure that goes slightly negative. That is the vasoplegic child who chatters the instant you approach target flow, and it happens with a completely normal total blood volume.

What changes about the plan

You have four levers, and only one of them is volume.

Add volume, which raises total distending volume. Reduce flow, which unwinds the redistribution. Give a venoconstrictor, which reduces venous rest volume and is the lever that addresses the actual pathology rather than the symptom. Or change the local geometry: reposition, upsize, or add a second drainage cannula.

The reason this matters is that reflexively volume-loading every chattering circuit is how a vasoplegic child ends up 3 litres positive by day two with the same chatter.

The four-lever problem has been run experimentally rather than only simulated. A running-hemodynamics analogue of mean systemic pressure was validated against true zero-flow measurement in porcine VA ECMO across vasoconstriction and volume-expansion states (ref 21), and an earlier study in the same model compared vasoconstriction with volume expansion as ways to raise circuit flow (ref 22). Neither replaces the judgment at the bedside. Both beat the reflex.

04Fontan circulation, CVP 18, low output, recurrent effusionsWhere the gradient language is closest to true, and still misleads+
The reflex answer

Pulmonary flow is driven by the transpulmonary gradient, so raise CVP with volume and you will drive more flow through the lungs.

What is actually happening

This is the honest hard case, because there genuinely is no pump between the systemic veins and the lungs, so a pressure difference really is what moves blood across that bed. But the energy still comes from one place. The single ventricle has to drive blood through the pulmonary and systemic resistances arranged in series, and everything it generates has to carry all the way around.

Adding volume raises CVP and therefore raises the transpulmonary gradient arithmetically. Whether it raises flow depends on whether the ventricle is preload responsive and on what pulmonary vascular resistance is doing. Past a point, more volume buys hepatic congestion, effusions, ascites and protein-losing enteropathy, and buys no output at all.

What changes about the plan

The levers that work are the ones that change the system rather than the reading. Lower pulmonary vascular resistance: keep mean airway pressure low, extubate to spontaneous negative-pressure breathing as early as it is safe, consider inhaled nitric oxide or a pulmonary vasodilator. Protect ventricular filling: sinus rhythm, AV valve competence, adequate diastolic time. Rule out anatomic obstruction in the pathway or a stenotic branch pulmonary artery before you accept a physiologic explanation.

And note the one place where the classical picture is more right than the critics: the respiratory pump adds real energy. Spontaneous negative-pressure ventilation genuinely augments Fontan flow, and Brengelmann explicitly exempts respiratory and skeletal muscle pumping from his argument. Extubating a Fontan is a hemodynamic intervention.

The closest modelling support for the first half of this case removed the right ventricle from a validated circulatory model and found pulmonary vascular resistance dominant, with volume expansion by any route of limited benefit (ref 29), which is the conclusion above arrived at from the other direction. For the ventilation half, see Magder and Lai on the hemodynamic consequences of positive-pressure ventilation (ref 30).

05Norepinephrine started in warm septic shock, cardiac output goes upA vasoconstrictor that increases flow is not a paradox+
The reflex answer

It squeezed the arteries and raised the pressure. The rise in output is a happy accident, or it came from recruiting unstressed volume out of a venous reservoir.

What is actually happening

Alpha-1 receptors are dense on splanchnic venous capacitance vessels, which are the biggest and most compliant part of the container. Contracting that smooth muscle reduces the resting size of the container. The same blood volume now occupies a smaller vascular space, so distending volume and distending pressure rise throughout, including in the segments that fill the right heart. If the ventricle is preload responsive, output rises despite the higher afterload.

Be precise about the mechanism, because the usual phrasing carries a picture that does not survive inspection. Nothing was released from a reserve tank. There is no pool of unstressed blood sitting apart from a stressed pool. The container changed shape, so the bookkeeping split between rest volume and distending volume changed, and blood genuinely did move between compartments as the system settled into its new state. Those are different claims.

Say that as a dispute about what the bookkeeping means, though, because that is what it is, and not as a fact most authors have wrong. Reservoir-and-recruitment language is standard in current reviews: Persichini and colleagues describe unstressed blood as a reservoir that can be recruited by endogenous or exogenous sympathetic stimulation (ref 14), and Møller and colleagues frame vasoconstriction on VA ECMO as recruitment of blood volume (ref 22). The argument here is that the recruitment is a change in the geometry of the container rather than the opening of a tap. That the volume moves is not in dispute.

What changes about the plan

In a cold, high-afterload child the same drug does the opposite: the afterload term dominates and output falls. So "does norepinephrine help or hurt here" is not a question about the drug. It is a question about where that particular ventricle sits, and you answer it by measuring output before and after, not by watching the mean arterial pressure improve.

There is a second reason not to chase the pressure here, and this page does not otherwise engage it. Pinsky and colleagues frame tissue perfusion in septic shock as a vascular waterfall whose upstream end is the arteriolar critical closing pressure and whose downstream end is mean systemic filling pressure. In vasoplegia the critical closing pressure falls toward Pmsf, the waterfall collapses, and autoregulation is abolished at a normal mean arterial pressure and a normal cardiac output (ref 34).

06Post-op day 1, CVP 18, cardiac index 1.8, pulsus on the arterial lineWhen a high filling pressure means the opposite of full+
The reflex answer

CVP is 18. This child is full. Stop the fluid and start an inotrope.

What is actually happening

The constraint is around the heart, not in the veins. Pericardial or mediastinal pressure is high, so the measured CVP is high while the transmural pressure actually distending the ventricle is low. The ventricle is a container being squeezed from outside; it cannot accept volume, and the CVP is reporting the squeeze rather than the filling. Every step of the venous return story is intact and irrelevant, because the failure is at the last compartment.

What changes about the plan

Fluid may help transiently, because it raises total distending volume faster than it raises pericardial pressure, and that is worth doing while you get to the answer. But the answer is drainage or reopening. The trap is letting a high CVP argue you out of the diagnosis, which is exactly what the back-pressure mental model encourages.

07Adolescent trauma, class III hemorrhageThe case where the classical picture is simply correct+
The reflex answer

Blood loss lowers mean systemic pressure, shifts the vascular function curve left, and the new intersection is lower output at lower filling pressure.

What is actually happening

Exactly that. Reduce total volume and total distending volume falls, every segment's distending pressure falls together, the curve shifts left at unchanged slope, and the crossing moves down and to the left. Guyton's diagram predicts this precisely, which is a large part of why it survived seventy years of criticism.

The only thing the newer reading changes is the sentence underneath. CVP fell because there is less blood distending the container, not because a driving gradient collapsed. Same prediction, different mechanism carried forward into cases 02 through 06 where the two accounts diverge.

What changes about the plan

Nothing. Give blood. This case is here to be fair to Guyton, and to make the point that a model can forecast correctly while explaining incorrectly. That is precisely why the wrong explanation survived: it kept getting the right answer.

VIICarry-out

What actually changes on rounds tomorrow

  1. CVP is a readout, not a knob. There is no intervention that sets it. It is where the heart's ability to clear volume meets the container's ability to hold it.
  2. A rising CVP with a flat stroke volume is a completed experiment. You bought congestion and no flow. That is the moment to stop, not to repeat.
  3. Fluid responsiveness is a property of the ventricle, not of the gradient. Where that ventricle sits on its Starling curve is the only thing being tested.
  4. Under positive pressure ventilation, measured CVP is not transmural CVP. The number can rise while the filling falls. Read trends against pleural pressure.
  5. Vasopressors act on the container, not just the pipes. That is why a vasoconstrictor can raise cardiac output, and it is the same physiology whether the effect is welcome or not.
  6. On ECMO, chatter is a volume-distribution problem with four possible fixes. Volume, flow, venous tone and cannula geometry. Reaching for volume every time is how children end up massively positive.
  7. Treat bedside Pms estimates as soft, and know roughly by how much. The maneuvers used to obtain them can alter the very properties they are meant to measure. Against right atrial balloon occlusion in pigs, the inspiratory-hold estimate overshot by 3.0 (SD 5.1) mmHg (ref 19); against zero-flow measurement, all three indirect estimates ran low, and volume status changed the error (ref 20).
  8. Keep the diagram, drop the verbs. The intersection is a good predictor. "Back pressure" and "the gradient drives return" are the parts that mislead.
VIIIEpistemics

What is settled and what is not

Settled

In both Guyton's and Levy's preparations, flow was imposed by an investigator and right atrial pressure was the response. Guyton wrote in 1955 that right atrial pressure is not one of the primary determinants of cardiac output but is itself determined simultaneously along with it (ref 2); the widely repeated 1979 reply to Levy is not indexed anywhere and this page no longer rests on it. The upstream variables are contractility, heart rate, resistance, capacitance and volume. Stressed and unstressed volume are a mathematical partition of one contained volume, not two anatomically separate pools.

Contested

Whether the (Pms − Pra) / RVR formulation should be abandoned. Magder defends the classical view, most recently in a 2025 review (ref 15) and in two 2024 papers on using the framework for fluid decisions (refs 16, 17). Møller and colleagues read a porcine heart-bypass preparation as supporting a back-pressure role (ref 18); Berger and colleagues' balloon-occlusion work is a separate design and is often cited as though it were the same one (ref 19). Brengelmann, Beard and Feigl argue for removing venous return curves from teaching entirely, and Sunagawa argues the opposite (ref 12). One thing has changed: the exchange has gone quiet on one side. No publication by Brengelmann is indexed after his 2021 reply, while Magder has published in 2024, 2025 and 2026.

If you want the strongest version of the opposing case, start with Magder's 2025 review (ref 15) rather than the 2006 Point, which is twenty years stale as a statement of his position. Pair it with Brengelmann's Counterpoint from the same 2006 issue (ref 9), then read the two porcine preparations side by side (refs 18, 19). This page argues the critics' side, and argues it hard. It is not the defence, and it should not be read as though the defence had been heard.

Numbers on this page that carry no citation

Three sets of figures here are derived from the models on this page rather than reported in any paper, and are labelled as derived wherever they appear.

1. The compliance of rigs 1 and 2. Total compliance is 175 mL/mmHg (8.75 arterial plus 166.25 venous in rig 1; 8.75 plus 140 plus 26.25 in rig 2). These are schematic appendix parameters, not human measurements, and they run roughly 2.5 times measured human systemic compliance, reported as 0.98 mL/mmHg/kg, about 69 mL/mmHg at 70 kg (ref 23), and as a median of 73 mL/mmHg, IQR 56 to 133 (ref 26). The 2000 mL default for distending volume is inside the measured range (1677 mL in ref 23; median 2029 mL in ref 26). The rigs' Pms of 11.4 mmHg is arithmetic on those two inputs, 2000 divided by 175, which is why the rigs sit below the 19 to 21 mmHg human bedside estimates. This is a disclosed approximation, not a reported value.

2. The pivot position. The figures of 31.4% of cumulative compliance at fixed vascular properties, and 28.2% to 32.8% under defended aortic pressure, are computed by rig 3. Reference 6 does not express a pivot or crossover point as a percentage of cumulative compliance, so no published number exists to check them against. This is a novel derivation from a published model, not a reproduction of a published result.

3. The "roughly twenty times larger" comparison in part II. That is arithmetic on the classical animal-preparation figures, 98 divided by 5. Levy's full text could not be retrieved when this page was revised, so those inputs are the numbers the argument is conventionally told with rather than values quoted from the paper. The 3 to 6 mmHg gradient half of the comparison is separately supported (ref 15); the 7 mmHg Pms half is not, and human bedside values are three times higher (refs 23 to 25).

One further gap is recorded rather than papered over: the classical claim that Rothe measured normal filling pressures of 7 to 10 mmHg could not be traced to Rothe. The 1993 abstract carries no numeric value and the full text was unreachable. The animal figure survives only indirectly, through ref 13.
