Five things that are just true

Levy noticed the problem in 1979, Rothe explained what the container was doing, Brengelmann closed it with conservation of energy. The argument is rebuilt here from five plain facts, four working rigs and seven PICU cases.

Nobody disputes the facts. The fight is over which of them causes which, and holding all five at once dissolves most of it.

01

The loop is closed, and the blood volume in it is fixed. Over the next thirty seconds no blood is created or destroyed; whatever piles up in one place came out of another.

02

Blood vessels are stretchy bags, not rigid pipes. A stretchy bag holds more when the pressure inside is higher. Veins are floppy and hold a lot; arteries are stiff and hold little.

03

There is one engine. The heart is the only thing continuously adding energy to the blood. (Breathing and leg muscles add some, and that matters later.)

04

Pushing fluid through a narrow place costs pressure. The drop is flow times resistance, and it is the price of the flow, not the reason for it.

05

In a steady state, what leaves the heart per minute enters it per minute. Cardiac output and venous return are one number measured in two places.

The one analogy to keep

You are riding a bicycle at 25 miles an hour and there is wind in your face. Two ways to describe it:

"There is wind in my face, therefore I am going fast."
"I am going fast, therefore there is wind in my face."

The second is right. The wind is the price of the speed; your legs are the engine. Riding into a gale doubles the wind and does not speed you up.

Every pressure difference in the circulation is that wind: real, measurable, proportional to flow, and not the engine.

ARig one

Two tanks and a pump

Before any physiology vocabulary, build the circulation out of two buckets: a narrow stiff one (the arteries) and a wide floppy one (the veins), joined by a narrow pipe (the resistance vessels). A pump lifts water from the floppy bucket into the stiff one; water leaks back through the pipe.

The total amount of water never changes. That is fact 01.

In a living animal the convergence is not this clean. Recording zero-flow pressures for ten minutes in 14 pigs, van Loon and colleagues found the equilibrium depended on how the arrest was produced and on residual reflexes: pentobarbital equilibrated quickly (mean arterial pressure 47 ± 3.7 to 16 ± 2.5 mmHg), while ventricular fibrillation left a persistent retrograde gradient of 5 ± 2.1 mmHg with central venous pressure above arterial. They conclude that mean circulatory filling pressure is not a stable, universal value (ref 27).

Two things to do before moving on. Set the pump to zero and watch both levels converge on the pressure the system settles to when flow stops. Then hold the pump steady and pull water out; every level falls together. Those are the only two ways to change that number, and they return as Rothe's central point.

IGuyton, 1955

A new gradient

Guyton faced a hard problem. The circulation is a closed loop, so every measurement contaminates every other: speed the heart up and the vessels change, change the vessels and the heart's filling changes. There is no clean place to stand.

His solution was to cut the loop. He replaced the heart with a mechanical pump, so the vascular system could be characterised on its own: set a flow, read the pressure at the right atrium, repeat.

The result was a straight line sloping down: at high flow right atrial pressure was low, at low flow higher, at zero flow everything equilibrated at one number, which he called the mean circulatory filling pressure. That number was not new; the insight was that it described the vessels themselves, independent of the heart.

VR = (MCFP − RAP) / ZVR

He then put that line on the same axes as Starling's cardiac function curve. Where they crossed was the only state both the heart and the vessels would accept: cardiac output and right atrial pressure were chosen by neither half, and fell out of the intersection.

What was brilliant

Neither the heart nor the circulation independently sets cardiac output. That was a real advance and is still why the diagram is taught. Change the volume and the vascular line shifts; change contractility and the cardiac curve shifts; the new crossing tells you where the patient lands.

Where it went wrong, in the same paper

The mathematics described a relationship; the words described a mechanism. Guyton wrote that right atrial pressure was not a primary determinant of cardiac output but was settled simultaneously with output by the interaction of heart and vessels. A few pages away he wrote that it was a back pressure opposing venous return, and that mean circulatory filling pressure promoted it.

It is quite obvious that the greater the right atrial pressure, the greater is the back pressure in the veins preventing the return of blood to the heart.

Guyton, Physiological Reviews 1955

That second sentence stuck because it is easy: a pressure difference, a resistance, a flow, and it sounds like a garden hose. A graph reads naturally as a causal story, but an axis is not an arrow. The direction of causation was never in the plot.

IILevy, 1979

The numerator strikes back

Levy did not attack the equation. He rewrote it, which is worse.

VR = (Pms − RAP) / RVR    becomes    Pms − RAP = VR × RVR

Identical algebra, opposite story: on the left a gradient produces a flow, on the right a flow produces a gradient. Nothing in the mathematics chooses between them.

The experiment does choose. In Guyton's preparation the investigator turned a knob on a mechanical pump and measured the pressure at the right atrium. Flow was set, pressure was read: the definition of an independent and a dependent variable, and the venous return curve had them backwards.

Where the pressures come from

Levy's answer is fact 01 and fact 02 together. The heart moves blood from the venous side to the arterial side; as flow rises arterial pressure rises, and because arteries are stretchy the arterial compartment holds more blood. Total volume is fixed, so that extra came out of the veins, and venous pressure falls with right atrial pressure.

Right atrial pressure limited nothing. It fell because the pump moved blood uphill and there was less left to distend the veins.

The disappearing gradient

Then Levy pointed at the numbers. Take mean systemic pressure at about 7 mmHg and right atrial pressure at about 2 mmHg; the famous gradient for venous return is 5 mmHg. The heart generated 100 mmHg at the aorta against 2 at the right atrium, roughly twenty times larger.

Read those as classical animal-preparation figures, not patient values. The 5 mmHg gradient holds up: Magder puts the difference between mean systemic filling pressure and right atrial pressure at 3 to 6 mmHg (ref 15). A Pms of 7 mmHg does not; human bedside estimates run roughly 19 to 21 mmHg, as part III sets out. The comparison is unaffected, since 98 over 5 is about twenty either way. Levy's full text could not be retrieved this run, so treat these as the figures the argument is conventionally told with rather than values quoted from the paper.

If the 5 mmHg gradient were the engine, raising right atrial pressure by 5 mmHg would stop the circulation. It does not; patients live at a central venous pressure of 15. The small number was never the energy source but a pressure difference created inside the circulation as blood passed through it.

The reply this page cannot source

The story usually told next is that the editors sent Levy's paper to Guyton, that his response ran alongside it, and that he conceded almost every conceptual point. No such reply is indexed. A PubMed author search returns nine Guyton items for 1979, none in Circulation Research and none about venous return, and Brengelmann's 2019 review lists no Guyton comment accompanying Levy 1979. The one 1979 Circulation Research defence that does exist is Alexander's, "In defense of 'venous return'" (ref 11). Levy's full text returned HTTP 403 with no open-access copy, so an appended editorial note can be neither confirmed nor ruled out. Until somebody produces it, this page does not rely on it.

What Guyton did put in print, and what the argument rests on instead, is in the 1955 review: right atrial pressure is not one of the primary determinants of cardiac output but is itself determined simultaneously along with it (ref 2). That is the concession the story wants, in his own words, twenty-four years earlier.

The point the story carries stands on its own. Levy's preparation made flow independent because an investigator held the knob; the intact heart holds no such knob and cannot impose an output. The upstream variables are contractility, heart rate, vascular resistance, capacitance and blood volume; output, venous return, arterial pressure and venous pressure are downstream, resolved together. Attribute that to the physiology, not to a letter nobody can produce.

The actual state of play after 1979. Both men were doing legitimate "what if" analyses: Guyton froze right atrial pressure to draw one curve, Levy froze flow to draw the other, and neither freezing describes the intact circulation. Pressure alone does not determine flow, flow alone does not determine pressure, and the system resolves them together.

IIIRothe, 1983 and 1993

Return of the elastic state

Pressure and flow are both outputs of something, and Rothe's answer is the shape of the container.

Three words people use interchangeably and should not

The vocabulary that makes the rest of it possible.
TermWhat it actually means
CapacityHow much blood is in that compartment right now. A number, in mL.
ComplianceThe slope, C = ΔV / ΔP: how much extra volume it takes to raise the pressure by one unit.
CapacitanceThe whole volume-pressure relationship, including where it starts. Two vessels can share a slope and be wildly different sizes.

Rothe's linear description of a vascular segment:

V = Vu + C × P

Vu is the unstressed or rest volume, how much the container holds when distending pressure is zero; C times P is the extra volume associated with stretching the wall.

The flat firehose

Take an empty firehose and start filling it. The cross section goes from squashed oval to round; the circumference never changes, so the wall never stretches and the pressure barely rises. That filling is the rest volume, and it is a lot: roughly three quarters of the blood in the body sits in vessels at or near this state. Berger and Takala put the stressed fraction at around 25% of total blood volume (ref 13), and Brengelmann's model, which rig 3 reimplements, assumes the same quarter (ref 6).

Only once the hose is round does adding more force the circumference open, stretch the wall and drive the pressure up. That extra is what people call stressed volume.

The correction almost everyone needs. Stressed and unstressed volume are not two pools of blood in separate compartments. Inside a pressurised vessel every millilitre is at the same pressure and equally under stress. The split is a bookkeeping line on one contained volume, separating the resting size of the container from the extra associated with stretching it. There is no hidden reserve tank waiting to be tapped.

What mean systemic pressure actually is

Pms = ΣVs / ΣC

Total stressed volume divided by total compliance. Not the pressure of any one vessel, and not the average of the pressures measured during flow, but a property of the whole elastic container: how full it is relative to how big it is.

Which gives two ways to raise it: put more blood in, or make the container smaller.

What the number measures out at

Animal zero-flow measurements sit below 10 mmHg; human bedside estimates do not. In mechanically ventilated post-cardiac-surgery patients, Pmsf by inspiratory hold was 18.8 ± 4.5 mmHg at baseline and rose to 29.1 ± 5.2 after 500 mL of colloid (ref 23). A later series in the same population gave Pmsf 20.9 ± 5.6, stop-flow arm pressure 19.8 ± 5.7 and the model analogue 14.9 ± 4.0 mmHg (ref 24).Erratum on record for PMID 22584797 (ref 24) — Intensive Care Med 2013;39(1):163. Linkage only, not retrieved; consult it before relying on these three figures. In healthy awake preoperative patients the stop-flow arm pressure was 22 ± 5 mmHg (ref 25). Berger and Takala's review puts living critically ill patients at 18 to 33 mmHg against animal values below 10 (ref 13).

Magder's 2025 review sets a target rather than reporting a measurement: mean systemic filling pressure should ideally sit under 10 mmHg to limit capillary drainage, with the gradient from it to right atrial pressure generally 3 to 6 mmHg (ref 15). The same number read as the upstream pressure for venous return is also the downstream pressure for capillary filtration, so a high one carries an edema cost whatever you believe about causation.

The classical "7 to 10 mmHg" range could not be traced to Rothe this run: his 1993 abstract reports no numeric value and the full text returned HTTP 403. The animal half is supported only indirectly, through ref 13; the human half is contradicted by refs 23 to 25.

Passive versus active, and why it matters clinically

Passive redistribution

The heart works harder, blood shifts from veins to arteries, venous volume and pressure fall. The veins recoil onto a lower point of their existing curve; their properties never changed, no venoconstriction happened, and Pms is unchanged.

Active venoconstriction

Venous smooth muscle contracts and the container itself changes, usually by reducing unstressed volume and sometimes compliance too. The same blood now sits at a higher pressure, so Pms rises with no change in blood volume.

Rothe's warning is that these look similar in an experiment and are frequently confused: a vascular bed that loses inflow will passively empty and can be mistaken for one that actively constricted.

The pivot, and the step too far

During flow, pressure falls from aorta to right atrium. Arterial pressure sits above Pms and right atrial pressure below it, so somewhere along the way the local pressure crosses the numerical value of Pms. Stop the heart and the compartments above that point lose volume while the ones below gain; near the crossover almost nothing moves. The name usually attached to it is the pivot pressure, usually attributed to Rothe. His 1993 abstract does not use the word, and Brengelmann's 2019 paper does not discuss a pivot; the phrasing turns up in Berger and Takala's 2018 review, in their own voice and unattributed (ref 13). As a descriptive marker it is fine, and because most volume and compliance live in the small veins and venules, that is usually where it falls.

Then he went further. Because the pressure in the small veins during flow happens to be close to Pms, he treated Pms as an estimate of their distending pressure, and so as the upstream pressure driving venous return. His 1993 abstract says exactly that.

A coincidence of magnitude promoted to a physical identity: an equilibrium property of the entire container given an anatomical address it does not have.

Real venous return curves flatten at strongly negative right atrial pressure because the great veins partially collapse and further falls change nothing upstream; that plateau is absent here by construction. Read the negative terminal pressures as the model saying the terminal segment has run out of volume, not as a pressure a vein would hold.

IVBrengelmann, 2003

The phantom pressure

Rothe had given Pms an address in the small veins. Brengelmann went back to the apparatus to ask whether anything lived there.

What the machine actually did

Guyton drained the right atrium through an external collapsible tube, a Starling resistor, into a mechanical pump. The pump delivered into the pulmonary artery and the left heart returned the same flow to the systemic circulation. An external reservoir was connected but closed while each curve was recorded, so the volume inside the peripheral vasculature stayed fixed. One flowmeter sat on the pump outflow, and every point was taken after a new steady state settled.

At steady state, pump output and cardiac output and venous return are necessarily the same number. The experiment never measured venous return as a flow distinct from cardiac output; it measured the steady flow through the vascular subsystem and the right atrial pressure that came with it.

Why the curve looks causal, and why that is a drawing convention

When you graph negative feedback between two subsystems, one of the two input-output relationships has to be plotted backwards, with its input variable on the vertical axis. In Guyton's diagram the venous return curve is the one drawn backwards. It has to be, or the curves could not share a set of axes.

The impression that right atrial pressure is doing something to flow is an artefact of the plotting convention that makes the intersection possible. Brengelmann's target is the claim it encourages: that raising right atrial pressure to the level of mean systemic pressure would stop venous return.

You cannot move right atrial pressure by itself

Raise the Starling resistor and right atrial pressure has to settle higher. But the right atrium and the nearby great veins must then hold more blood at that higher pressure, and total volume is fixed, so that blood comes from upstream: the distribution shifts and flow cannot stay where it was. Whether Guyton reset the pump by hand or the collapsing tube throttled its filling does not matter. Either way the recorded point is the flow and pressure jointly compatible with the resistor height, the vascular properties and the fixed blood volume.

The ledger experiment

Brengelmann then proposed a different apparatus that produces the same curve. Take out the Starling resistor; let blood from the right atrium spill through an open tube into an external reservoir, the tube height setting right atrial pressure, with a pump drawing from the reservoir back to the pulmonary artery. The reservoir is not an energy source but a visible ledger of how much blood has entered or left the peripheral vasculature.

  1. Stop the pump. Set the tube so right atrial pressure equals Pms. Everything equilibrates and the vasculature holds a reference volume, V0.
  2. Start the pump at some flow with the tube unmoved. Upstream pressures rise above Pms and those compartments expand, so the vasculature holds more than V0. The extra came out of the reservoir.
  3. Lower the tube. Right atrial pressure falls, outflow briefly exceeds inflow, and blood drains back into the reservoir until the vasculature holds V0 again.
  4. You now have one point: a flow, and the right atrial pressure that goes with it at volume V0. Repeat at a new flow; the order can be reversed with no difference.

Trace the points and you get Guyton's curve. Nothing in the apparatus was ever held at Pms. It took paired changes in flow and right atrial pressure, real segmental pressure differences, compliant compartments, conservation of blood volume, and a return to the same vascular volume at every point.

The equation survives. The picture does not.

Brengelmann then ran a three-compartment model with an ideal pump. At zero flow all three compartments sat at Pms; the moment flow started none did, arterial pressure rising above it and both venous pressures falling below, with volume moving accordingly. The model still produced the familiar near-linear flow against right atrial pressure relationship, and its algebra still rearranged into flow equals (Pms minus right atrial pressure) over an effective resistance.

Pms is in that derivation for one reason: total vascular volume was stipulated fixed, and at zero flow that volume sits across the combined compliances at one common pressure. Pms is the compact way of writing the constraint. The derivation never found a vessel held at Pms; it introduced the zero-flow pressure while accounting for volume under flow.

The denominator misleads in the same way. Once Pms sat in the small veins, the resistance to venous return read as a physical venous resistance between it and the right atrium. It is not; it is a composite of arterial and venous resistances with how compliance is distributed across the network, which is why Guyton first called it an impedance.

So what is the phantom

Local pressure gradients are real. Neighbouring segments hold different pressures at the same instant, blood passes through real resistive pathways, and pressure falls along them as energy dissipates. None of that is in question.

The gradient from Pms to right atrial pressure has a different status. Right atrial pressure is a local pressure in a flowing circulation; Pms is the common pressure reached when flow stops. The equation sets them side by side as though they were opposite ends of one pathway. This is not water running downhill, where both points exist at once and gravity releases stored energy. In the circulation the heart supplies the energy and sets up the flowing profile.

The chain, and where it breaks. A local venular pressure numerically approximates Pms. That pressure is therefore called Pms. Pms is then assumed to persist there during flow, and therefore to supply an upstream driving pressure opposed by right atrial pressure across a resistance to venous return. Only the first step follows from the data.

The pivot keeps its descriptive value. It marks the crossover between regions that gain and lose volume as the circulation changes state, and sits among the small veins because that is where the volume and the compliance are. But a crossover that moves is not an equilibrium pressure with an address. The pivot is real as a crossing point, the pressure real as a local pressure; the phantom is the identity assigned to it.

One loose end. Brengelmann's pump imposed flow, so the model shows how a fixed volume redistributes at different flow rates without asking how much flow a real heart could sustain. If Pms is not a pressure source, why does the elastic state of the circulation so obviously constrain achievable output? That is where the other side makes its case, most recently in Magder's 2025 review (ref 15), not the 2006 Point.

VBrengelmann, 2019

The energy argument

Everything above is about interpretation. This argument is about physics, and Brengelmann made it central sixteen years after the 2003 paper.

Picture the standard diagram: an elastic bag at Pms, draining through a resistance against a back pressure at the right atrium. Where does the energy for that outflow come from? The bag stores energy in its stretched walls, but to release it the bag would have to shrink, and it is not shrinking. It is refilled at exactly the rate it drains, at constant volume and pressure.

A container held at fixed volume and fixed pressure cannot supply the work of driving its own outflow. It is a passive element in the pathway, and whatever drives its inflow is the engine. That is the heart.

The symmetry in the algebra

Guyton's expression comes from one algebraic step: write every compartment pressure as right atrial pressure plus the intervening drops, then impose constant total volume. You get (Pms − Pra) / F on one side and a ratio of resistance-times-compliance terms on the other, which has the units of resistance. Call it the resistance to venous return.

Now write the same compartment pressures as arterial pressure minus the intervening drops. Same constraint, same algebra, and you get:

F = (Pa − Pms) / RAD

Equally valid. Equally derivable. Had Guyton plotted flow against arterial pressure, we would have spent seventy years teaching that the way to raise cardiac output is to lower Pms, the back pressure opposing arterial pressure. Nobody says that, because it sounds absurd. It is as absurd as the version we do teach.

The appearance of Pms in either expression has nothing to do with what drives flow. It is there because you stipulated that total volume is constant, and Pms is total distending volume divided by total compliance. Bookkeeping.

The fair caveats

  1. The diagram still predicts well. Bleed a patient and the vascular curve shifts left at unchanged slope; the new intersection is lower output at lower filling pressure. That is right, and the objection is to the mechanism, not the forecast.
  2. Acutely dropping right atrial pressure does transiently raise venous return. Brengelmann grants this, and it is why we keep a separate word for venous return. What he denies is that the imbalance persists into the new steady state.
  3. Muscle pumps are real engines. Respiratory and skeletal muscle pumping genuinely add energy; the criticism is aimed narrowly at steady-state elastic recoil at fixed volume.
  4. The argument is live, and the other side has the more recent papers. Magder's 2006 Point holds that the classical Guyton view is correct, and his 2025 review restates it (ref 15). Two porcine preparations that answer different questions get run together in support of it and should not be, as part VIII sets out (refs 18, 19). Beard and Feigl argue the concept should be removed from teaching outright; Sunagawa argues it should be taught, conceding the causal point and denying that it matters, since at constant stressed volume venous return and venous pressure hold a fixed reciprocal relationship (ref 12). This is not settled.
DRig four

What actually changed?

Everything above is about where flow and pressure come from. This section is the bedside version: you did something, the numbers moved, and you have to decide whether to do it again. Two questions get most of the way there. What did you change, the contents or the container? And what did the change become, flow or pressure?

Contents and container

Give fluid and there is more blood in the system than a minute ago. Give a vasoconstrictor and there is as much blood as before, in a container whose pressure-volume behaviour has changed, so the blood redistributes. Both routes can raise central filling, cardiac output, arterial pressure, central venous pressure and congestion, and the bedside phenotype does not tell you which one you took.

They are not interchangeable. Fluid adds to the total; tone rearranges a total that is already fixed, and there is a limit to how much rearranging is available. Part III's vocabulary table makes the difference sayable: compliance is a slope, and the full pressure-volume relationship also fixes where that slope starts. The whole relationship, not the slope alone, decides how much blood a segment holds at a given transmural pressure, answering to vessel size, wall stiffness, smooth-muscle tone, the pressure outside the vessel and how full the segment already is. Two segments can share a compliance and hold very different volumes at the same pressure, and venous smooth muscle can change how much a vein holds while barely touching its slope.

Venoconstriction does not pump

The picture most of us carry is of veins squeezing blood back toward the heart. Nothing sustains the squeezing. Constriction changes the container, blood redistributes over some seconds, and then it is done: the new steady state has veins holding less blood at a higher pressure, not veins doing continuous work. It is a one-off shift in where the blood sits, not a second engine, and part V is the general form of the argument: a container held at constant volume and pressure supplies no work.

Cardiopulmonary acceptance

Rigs 1 to 3 are all container. They show how a fixed quantity of blood redistributes across an elastic network at different flows, and are silent on how much flow a real heart would produce from any given filling state. The heart is a throughput organ: it does not bank what arrives, it either passes it on or it does not.

Whether extra systemic delivery becomes flow or pressure depends on how much the cardiopulmonary unit will accept, a composite: ventricular filling characteristics, contractile reserve, systolic capacity, the load on each ventricle, the pulmonary circulation the right ventricle pumps into, pericardial constraint, ventricular interaction inside that pericardium, the pressure surrounding the heart, any obstruction to inflow or outflow, and rhythm and rate.

Good acceptance

Chamber volume rises a little, filling pressure rises a little, Frank-Starling is recruited, stroke volume and output rise. The delivery became flow.

Poor acceptance

Little extra gets through. Blood accumulates upstream of whatever limits throughput; right atrial and systemic venous pressure rise, and congestion with them. The delivery became pressure.

Same delivered change, either way. The heart decides which you get, and the rig below puts both axes on one picture.

The acceptance settings are three positions on rig 2's schematic Starling relationship, not a model of any mechanism; the tone slider moves venous compliance and rest volume only, with arteriolar resistance held fixed. The provenance note under the references gives the numbers.

Where Pms sits in all of this

blood volume + vascular pressure-volume behaviour → systemic elastic state → Pms

Read the arrows as "is described by", not as "drives". Pms is not a mechanism and not an energy source pushing blood through a venous resistance. It is a reduced-order descriptor of the elastic state of the systemic vasculature, how full the container is relative to how big it is, which is why part III derives it as total stressed volume over total compliance. It describes the delivery state; it does not determine cardiac output. Flow appears where that state meets an acceptance, which is why rig 4's operating point sits at a crossing and never on either curve alone.

Two consequences, both argued here already from a different direction. Venous return is not a causal step between the vessels and the heart: at steady state it and cardiac output are one flow read from two sides, which is fact 05. And stressed and unstressed volume are a reduced-order model, not two anatomical pools.

Noradrenaline does more than one thing

The chain we teach is noradrenaline, then venoconstriction, then venous return up. It is less wrong than incomplete. Inside the same minute the drug shifts venous pressure-volume behaviour, raises arteriolar resistance, changes arterial pressure-volume behaviour, raises the afterload both ventricles work against, can raise contractility through beta-1, can improve coronary perfusion when the starting pressure was low enough to matter, and provokes reflexes to all of it. Those effects pull cardiac output in different directions, and which wins depends on the state the circulation was in before the drug arrived, which is why the same infusion raises output in one patient and lowers it in the next. Rig 4's tone slider models one effect and rig 2 has a slider for another; neither models the rest, and neither should be read as a model of the drug.

VIBedside

Seven PICU cases, worked

Each has the reflex answer most of us were taught, what is physically happening instead, and what changes about the plan.

01Six-month-old in septic shock, 20 mL/kg over 15 minutesCVP goes 4 to 9. What did the bolus do?+
The reflex answer

The bolus raised mean systemic pressure, widening the gradient for venous return, so more blood came back and output rose.

What is actually happening

You added volume to a container of fixed size, so total distending volume rose. Nearly all the compliance is on the venous side, so nearly all the new volume landed there and raised venous distending pressure, including in the segments that fill the right ventricle. Right ventricular end-diastolic volume rose.

Whether output rises depends on one thing: where that ventricle sits on its own Starling curve. The heart converts filling into flow. The bolus did not push blood forward; it made more available for a ventricle that may or may not use it.

What changes about the plan

Measure the output, not the filling pressure. If CVP rises and stroke volume does not, you bought congestion rather than flow, and should stop. Then look downstream for the cost: portal vein pulsatility, hepatic and renal venous Doppler, urine output over the next several hours. A rising CVP with a flat stroke volume is the clearest signal that the next bolus is a bad idea.

Read the Doppler and the filling pressure as complementary, not interchangeable. A 2026 review makes the distinction cleanly: CVP is a continuous readout of the risk of congestion, VExUS an intermittent organ-level look at congestion that has already happened, and neither substitutes for the other (ref 31).

02Pediatric ARDS, PEEP up from 8 to 14, CVP 8 to 14, cardiac index fallsThe number went up and the flow went down. Why?+
The reflex answer

PEEP raised right atrial pressure, which opposes mean systemic pressure, so the gradient narrowed and venous return fell.

What is actually happening

Three separate things, none of them a back pressure.

First, juxtacardiac pressure rose. Measured CVP went up while the transmural pressure that distends the right ventricle went down: the number rose and the filling fell, at the same time, for the same reason.

Second, the intrathoracic great veins are among the most compliant and lowest-pressure segments on the staircase. Squeezing them from outside shrinks the container where the margin is thinnest, pushing volume out of the chest into the abdomen and periphery.

Third, if any lung is now overdistended, pulmonary vascular resistance rises and right ventricular afterload with it: a separate mechanism, often the dominant one.

What changes about the plan

Stop reading absolute CVP under high PEEP; read the trend against pleural pressure. Ask whether the PEEP step is recruiting or overdistending by looking at driving pressure and compliance at each step; if compliance stopped improving and output fell, the PEEP bought nothing and cost flow.

It also explains why bedside estimation of Pms from inspiratory hold maneuvers is shakier than it looks. Brengelmann's caution is that the maneuver may change segmental rest volumes and compliances on both sides of the diaphragm, which changes Pms by definition, and may recruit reflex changes in segmental resistance. In his simulation, adjusting arteriolar resistance alone shifts both the extrapolated Pms and the apparent slope, with no other resistance in the model changed.

That is no longer only a simulation. Measured in pigs against right atrial balloon occlusion, the inspiratory-hold estimate overshot true mean systemic filling pressure by 3.0 (SD 5.1) mmHg, and raising PEEP from 5 to 10 raised the true value from 12.9 to 14.0 mmHg with no change in flow (ref 19). A later study in the same laboratory found all three indirect estimates ran low against zero-flow measurement, with volume status changing the error (ref 20).

03VA ECMO circuit chatter at target flowThe single best demonstration in the whole argument+
The reflex answer

The patient is hypovolemic. Give volume.

What is actually happening

On VA ECMO you are Guyton's preparation: you hold the pump knob and right atrial pressure is the reading. The causal direction is not in dispute; it is in your hand.

Raise the flow and volume redistributes uphill toward the arterial side, as in rig 3. The last step, the cavae at your drainage cannula, has the lowest distending pressure and the smallest margin, so it empties first. When its distending pressure reaches zero the vessel changes shape, the geometry interferes with flow, and you get chatter.

The model puts numbers on it. In the reference state the central venous compartment holds 233 mL of distending volume. Vasodilate the venous bed, which is what sepsis and most of your sedation does, and that falls to 37 mL; push the flow back up to restore an aortic pressure of 136 and it falls to 10 mL, with a computed terminal pressure that goes slightly negative. That is the vasoplegic child who chatters as you approach target flow, at a normal total blood volume.

What changes about the plan

Four levers, and only one is volume.

Add volume, raising total distending volume. Reduce flow, unwinding the redistribution. Give a venoconstrictor, which reduces venous rest volume and addresses the pathology rather than the symptom. Or change the geometry: reposition, upsize, add a second drainage cannula.

Reflexively volume-loading every chattering circuit is how a vasoplegic child ends up 3 litres positive by day two with the same chatter.

The four-lever problem has been run experimentally, not only simulated. A running-hemodynamics analogue of mean systemic pressure was validated against true zero-flow measurement in porcine VA ECMO across vasoconstriction and volume-expansion states (ref 21), and an earlier study in the same model compared the two as ways to raise circuit flow (ref 22). Neither replaces bedside judgment; both beat the reflex.

04Fontan circulation, CVP 18, low output, recurrent effusionsWhere the gradient language is closest to true, and still misleads+
The reflex answer

Pulmonary flow is driven by the transpulmonary gradient, so raise CVP with volume and more flow goes through the lungs.

What is actually happening

This is the honest hard case: there is no pump between the systemic veins and the lungs, so a pressure difference really is what moves blood across that bed. But the energy still comes from one place. The single ventricle drives blood through the pulmonary and systemic resistances in series, and everything it generates must carry all the way around.

Adding volume raises CVP and so raises the transpulmonary gradient arithmetically. Whether it raises flow depends on whether the ventricle is preload responsive and on pulmonary vascular resistance. Past a point, more volume buys hepatic congestion, effusions, ascites and protein-losing enteropathy, and no output at all.

What changes about the plan

The levers that work change the system rather than the reading. Lower pulmonary vascular resistance: keep mean airway pressure low, extubate to spontaneous negative-pressure breathing as early as is safe, consider inhaled nitric oxide or a pulmonary vasodilator. Protect ventricular filling: sinus rhythm, AV valve competence, adequate diastolic time. Rule out anatomic obstruction or a stenotic branch pulmonary artery before accepting a physiologic explanation.

Note the one place where the classical picture is more right than the critics: the respiratory pump adds real energy. Spontaneous negative-pressure ventilation genuinely augments Fontan flow, and Brengelmann exempts respiratory and skeletal muscle pumping. Extubating a Fontan is a hemodynamic intervention.

The closest modelling support removed the right ventricle from a validated circulatory model and found pulmonary vascular resistance dominant, with volume expansion by any route of limited benefit (ref 29), the conclusion above arrived at from the other direction. For the ventilation half, see Magder and Lai on the hemodynamic consequences of positive-pressure ventilation (ref 30).

05Norepinephrine started in warm septic shock, cardiac output goes upA vasoconstrictor that increases flow is not a paradox+
The reflex answer

It squeezed the arteries and raised the pressure. The rise in output is a happy accident, or came from recruiting unstressed volume out of a venous reservoir.

What is actually happening

Alpha-1 receptors are dense on splanchnic venous capacitance vessels, the biggest and most compliant part of the container. Contracting that smooth muscle reduces the container's resting size, so the same blood volume occupies a smaller space and distending volume and pressure rise throughout, including in the segments that fill the right heart. If the ventricle is preload responsive, output rises despite the higher afterload.

The usual phrasing carries a picture that does not survive inspection. Nothing was released from a reserve tank; there is no pool of unstressed blood apart from a stressed pool. The container changed shape, so the bookkeeping split between rest and distending volume changed, and blood genuinely did move between compartments as the system settled. Those are different claims.

Say that as a dispute about what the bookkeeping means, not as a fact most authors have wrong. Reservoir-and-recruitment language is standard in current reviews: Persichini and colleagues describe unstressed blood as a reservoir recruitable by endogenous or exogenous sympathetic stimulation (ref 14), and Møller and colleagues frame vasoconstriction on VA ECMO as recruitment of blood volume (ref 22). The argument here is that the recruitment is a change in the container's geometry, not the opening of a tap. That the volume moves is not in dispute.

What changes about the plan

In a cold, high-afterload child the same drug does the opposite: the afterload term dominates and output falls. "Does norepinephrine help or hurt here" is not a question about the drug but about where that ventricle sits, and you answer it by measuring output before and after, not by watching mean arterial pressure improve.

There is a second reason not to chase the pressure here, one this page does not otherwise engage. Pinsky and colleagues frame tissue perfusion in septic shock as a vascular waterfall running from the arteriolar critical closing pressure down to mean systemic filling pressure. In vasoplegia the critical closing pressure falls toward Pmsf, the waterfall collapses, and autoregulation is abolished at normal mean arterial pressure and normal cardiac output (ref 34).

06Post-op day 1, CVP 18, cardiac index 1.8, pulsus on the arterial lineWhen a high filling pressure means the opposite of full+
The reflex answer

CVP is 18. This child is full. Stop the fluid, start an inotrope.

What is actually happening

The constraint is around the heart, not in the veins. Pericardial or mediastinal pressure is high, so measured CVP is high while the transmural pressure distending the ventricle is low. The ventricle is a container squeezed from outside; it cannot accept volume, and the CVP reports the squeeze, not the filling. Every step of the venous return story is intact and irrelevant, because the failure is at the last compartment.

What changes about the plan

Fluid may help transiently, because it raises total distending volume faster than pericardial pressure, and is worth doing while you get to the answer. But the answer is drainage or reopening. The trap is letting a high CVP argue you out of the diagnosis, which is what the back-pressure model encourages.

07Adolescent trauma, class III hemorrhageThe case where the classical picture is simply correct+
The reflex answer

Blood loss lowers mean systemic pressure and shifts the vascular function curve left, so the new intersection is lower output at lower filling pressure.

What is actually happening

Exactly that. Reduce total volume and total distending volume falls, every segment's distending pressure falls together, the curve shifts left at unchanged slope, and the crossing moves down and left. Guyton's diagram predicts this precisely, much of why it survived seventy years of criticism.

The newer reading changes only the sentence underneath. CVP fell because there is less blood distending the container, not because a driving gradient collapsed. Same prediction, different mechanism, and it is the mechanism that carries into cases 02 through 06 where the accounts diverge.

What changes about the plan

Nothing. Give blood. This case is here to be fair to Guyton: a model can forecast correctly while explaining incorrectly, which is why the wrong explanation survived. It kept getting the right answer.

VIICarry-out

What actually changes on rounds tomorrow

  1. CVP is a readout, not a knob. No intervention sets it. It is where the heart's ability to clear volume meets the container's ability to hold it.
  2. A rising CVP with a flat stroke volume is a completed experiment. You bought congestion and no flow. That is the moment to stop, not to repeat. Congestion is also not the same as excess total body volume: it can come from volume, the container, poor acceptance, or any combination, so reaching for the diuretic every time answers one third of it.
  3. Fluid responsiveness is a property of the ventricle, not of the gradient. Where that ventricle sits on its Starling curve is the only thing tested; a positive test names the response, not the cause, and the treatment does not follow from the test alone.
  4. Under positive pressure ventilation, measured CVP is not transmural CVP. The number can rise while filling falls. Read trends against pleural pressure.
  5. Vasopressors act on the container, not just the pipes. That is why a vasoconstrictor can raise cardiac output, and the physiology is the same whether the effect is welcome or not.
  6. On ECMO, chatter is a volume-distribution problem with four fixes. Volume, flow, venous tone and cannula geometry. Reaching for volume every time is how children end up massively positive.
  7. Treat bedside Pms estimates as soft, and know roughly by how much. The maneuvers can alter the properties they are meant to measure. Against right atrial balloon occlusion in pigs, the inspiratory-hold estimate overshot by 3.0 (SD 5.1) mmHg (ref 19); against zero-flow measurement, all three indirect estimates ran low, and volume status changed the error (ref 20).
  8. Before you repeat it, name what changed and what it became. Fluid changes the contents; a vasoconstrictor changes the container. Then ask whether the change showed up as flow or as pressure. Those two questions separate patients whose numbers look identical, and decide whether the next dose is treatment or congestion.
  9. Keep the diagram, drop the verbs. The intersection predicts well. "Back pressure" and "the gradient drives return" are the parts that mislead.
VIIIEpistemics

What is settled and what is not

Settled

In both Guyton's and Levy's preparations, flow was imposed by an investigator and right atrial pressure was the response. Guyton wrote in 1955 that right atrial pressure is not one of the primary determinants of cardiac output but is itself determined simultaneously along with it (ref 2); the widely repeated 1979 reply to Levy is not indexed anywhere, and this page does not rest on it. The upstream variables are contractility, heart rate, resistance, capacitance and volume. Stressed and unstressed volume are a mathematical partition of one contained volume, not two separate pools.

Contested

Whether the (Pms − Pra) / RVR formulation should be abandoned. Magder defends the classical view, most recently in a 2025 review (ref 15) and two 2024 papers on using the framework for fluid decisions (refs 16, 17). Møller and colleagues read a porcine heart-bypass preparation as supporting a back-pressure role (ref 18); Berger and colleagues' balloon-occlusion work is a separate design, often cited as though it were the same one (ref 19). Brengelmann, Beard and Feigl argue for removing venous return curves from teaching entirely; Sunagawa argues the opposite (ref 12). The exchange has also gone quiet on one side: no Brengelmann publication is indexed after his 2021 reply, while Magder has published in 2024, 2025 and 2026.

For the strongest version of the opposing case, start with Magder's 2025 review (ref 15) rather than the 2006 Point, twenty years stale as a statement of his position. Pair it with Brengelmann's Counterpoint from the same 2006 issue (ref 9), then read the two porcine preparations side by side (refs 18, 19). This page argues the critics' side, and argues it hard. It is not the defence, and should not be read as though the defence had been heard.

Numbers on this page that carry no citation

Three sets of figures here are derived from the models on this page rather than reported in any paper, and are labelled as derived wherever they appear.

1. The compliance of rigs 1 and 2. Total compliance is 175 mL/mmHg (8.75 arterial plus 166.25 venous in rig 1; 8.75 plus 140 plus 26.25 in rig 2). The 2000 mL default for distending volume is inside the measured range (1677 mL in ref 23; median 2029 mL in ref 26). The rigs' Pms of 11.4 mmHg is 2000 divided by 175, which is why they sit below the 19 to 21 mmHg human bedside estimates. A disclosed approximation, not a reported value.

2. The pivot position. The figures of 31.4% of cumulative compliance at fixed vascular properties, and 28.2% to 32.8% under defended aortic pressure, are computed by rig 3. Reference 6 does not express a pivot or crossover point as a percentage of cumulative compliance, so no published number exists to check them against. A novel derivation from a published model, not a reproduction of a published result.

3. The "roughly twenty times larger" comparison in part II. Arithmetic on the classical animal-preparation figures, 98 divided by 5. Levy's full text could not be retrieved when this page was revised, so those inputs are the numbers the argument is conventionally told with rather than values quoted from the paper. 4. Everything rig 4 reports. Rig 4 has no published counterpart.
