Above PIF, excess gas flushes aerosol out through the nares. Nebulizer Compatibility and Placement by HFNC Platform

“Vibrating mesh versus jet” is only half the question at the bedside. The other half is which HFNC platform the nebulizer is plugged into, because the two systems in common use differ by roughly an order of magnitude in bench aerosol delivery even when both are running the same Aerogen mesh nebulizer with the same drug. Everything in this section is taken from the manufacturers’ own instructions for use or from the studies’ own methods sections; where a claim could not be sourced, it is marked rather than asserted.

Vapotherm Circuit Design and the Aerosol Delivery Gap

The design differs, and two studies measured where the drug goes. MANUFACTURER The Precision Flow IFU describes the delivery limb as follows: “The warmed humidified gas passes through the center of a triple-lumen heated delivery tube. The center lumen is surrounded by two outer lumens circulating warmed water to maintain the temperature of the inner lumen and to minimize rain-out. A proprietary short nasal cannula is connected to the end of the delivery tube.” Humidification is done by a vapor transfer cartridge rather than a pass-over chamber. There is a high-flow cartridge for 5–40 L/min and a low-flow cartridge for 1–8 L/min, with patients under 3 kg required to be on low flow.

Where the dose actually went. Perry 2013 reported that “most (62–80%) of the loaded albuterol dose accumulated within the adaptor.” DiBlasi 2024 quantified the same problem and its trade-off: impactive losses in the proximal aerosol adapter were 56%, but that adapter contained the losses better than the distal aerosol circuit (less cannula sputter, 5% vs 22%; fewer fugitive losses, 18% vs 24%; and a smaller inhaled particle, 5 µm vs 13 µm), while the distal circuit gave marginally greater lung dose in the pediatric and adult models and no difference in the neonatal model.

What is not established. PHYSIOLOGIC REASONING That the triple-lumen tube and the proprietary short small-bore cannula cause the order-of-magnitude gap is a plausible reading of the impaction data, not a tested claim; no study in this reference list isolates the tube, the cartridge, or the cannula as the responsible component. Treat the gap as a measured device-level difference, not as a mechanism you can quote.

And the gap does not carry over to work of breathing. The one clinical head-to-head compared both systems in children under 3 years and found no significant difference in the reduction in pressure·rate product between them (p = .12), with effort of breathing falling most at 1.5–2.0 L/kg/min on either platform (Weiler 2017, PMID 28669609). A platform can be the weaker aerosol route and the equal respiratory-support route at the same time; do not let one finding stand in for the other.

Worked Cases of Peak Inspiratory Flow by Age

Every ratio in Figure B depends on a number most readers have no feel for. These cases give it a value. Each card states whether its inspiratory flow was MEASURED in patients, DERIVED from a published breathing-model setting, or EXTRAPOLATED past the evidence. The ratios are computed from the number shown, not typed in.

The two “peak inspiratory flow” conventions are not interchangeable, and this tool mixes them because the literature does. Li 2019, whose regression is the line in Figure B, defines inspiratory flow as tidal volume divided by inspiratory time: its quiet-breathing patterns of 300, 500 and 700 mL over a 1.33 s inspiratory time are reported as 13.5, 22.5 and 31.5 L/min, which is VT/TI exactly. Milesi 2021 instead measured a true spirometric peak in real infants. For any flow profile that is not a square wave the true peak is the larger of the two (for a sinusoidal profile, by a factor of π/2 ≈ 1.57). A ratio built on a measured peak is therefore smaller than the same patient’s ratio built on VT/TI, and the two cannot be pooled. Cards below state which convention each number uses.

Key Principles

1. Absolute flow is not the governing variable. Raising HFNC flow does lower delivery when the patient's inspiratory flow is held constant, but the ratio of gas flow to inspiratory flow is what governs. With a distressed adult breathing pattern the inhaled dose at 10 L/min was lower than at 30 and 50 L/min (Dailey 2017, p=.009 and p=.01), and in that same adult in vitro model the gas flow producing the maximum inhaled dose shifted from 10 L/min (20.8 ± 0.7%, at a simulated inspiratory flow of 27 L/min) to 20 L/min (22.0 ± 0.6% and 26.7 ± 0.7%, at simulated inspiratory flows of 42 and 52.5 L/min). The peak tracks inspiratory flow; it is not a property of the flow setting alone (Li 2019). Delivery efficiency also rises with age across a matched model series (0.2–0.8% neonatal, 0.2–2.2% small child, 0.5–5.2% adult) despite far higher absolute flows in the adult (DiBlasi 2024). The “most loss between 2–20 LPM” framing holds only for small children, whose inspiratory flows are low.

2. The HFNC:PIF ratio is the primary predictor, and lower is better than equal. The ratio was the primary independent predictor of inhaled dose in an adult in vitro model, with the model explaining 78.8% of the variance (Li 2019). Delivery is maximized when cannula flow is below peak inspiratory flow, not equal to it: the plateau of maximum delivery sits at a ratio of roughly 0.1–0.5. The only randomized clinical comparison of ratios was null on the endpoint its authors call robust. In 75 adults with documented bronchodilator reversibility randomized to GF:IF = 0.5, GF:IF = 1.0, or a fixed 50 L/min, the standard ATS/ERS response criterion (FEV1 rise of 200 mL and 12%) showed no difference between flows at any cumulative dose: 64% vs 58% vs 42% at 1.5 mg (p=0.271) and 68% vs 75% vs 69% at 3.5 mg (p=0.848). The widely quoted 64% vs 29% vs 27% (p=0.011) is the same trial reported by a second, more sensitive responder definition the authors applied alongside the first: post-bronchodilator FEV1 via HFNC returning to the screening post-bronchodilator level (Li 2021, PMID 34683948). Earlier versions of this tool printed only the second number. Both belong here, and the honest reading is hypothesis-generating, not demonstrated. A 2026 graded evidence summary of the adult literature lands in the same place, suggesting flow at about 50% of inspiratory flow (Level 2b, Grade A; Xie 2026). But it inherits this trial, so it is not independent confirmation.

3. Vibrating mesh outperforms jet during HFNC, by roughly 2–3×. The magnitude is stated directly in the Li & Fink 2021 review and is consistent with the head-to-head SPECT-CT comparison (3.6% vs 1.0% at 30 L/min, Dugernier 2017); Ari 2019 reported a two- to fourfold advantage across HFNC, facemask and mouthpiece. The platform you plug that mesh nebulizer into moves delivery further than the mesh-versus-jet choice does: 0.9–2.5% via Vapotherm against 12.6–18.2% via Optiflow in the same adult manikin with the same nebulizer at the same position (Alanazi & Li 2023, PMID 37553215). See Which Platform, Which Nebulizer, and Where It Sits above. That comparison has been made at a small number of discrete flows, so it is not established “at every flow rate.” The 2023 international consensus prefers a mesh nebulizer over a jet nebulizer during HFNC and notes the advantage is independent of flow and FIO2; a jet nebulizer also adds driving gas that alters the delivered flow.

4. Practical steps at high flow.

  • Nebulizer position, and the low-flow exception the headline hides. What “the inlet of the humidifier” means depends on the platform: on Optiflow it is the AIRVO Neb adapter built into the water chamber; on Vapotherm it is the Aerosol Disposable Patient Circuit’s mixing chamber at the humidifier gas outlet, as opposed to the proximal Aerogen Aerosol Adapter that sits between the gas outlet and the cannula. See the device section above. At HFNC flows ≥ 10 L/min, place the vibrating mesh nebulizer at the inlet of the humidifier. That flow threshold is part of the recommendation, not a footnote to it: the 2023 consensus statement reads “nebulizers are recommended to be placed at the inlet of humidifier at HFNC flows ≥ 10 L/min” (perfect consensus, strong recommendation), and placement before the humidifier increased deposition in both Ari 2011 and Sunbul 2015. Below 10 L/min the consensus does not apply and offers no guidance. That is the 2–5 and 5–10 L/min bands in the table above, which is where most infants and toddlers actually sit. There the direction reverses: Li and Fink 2021 write that the nebulizer should go at the humidifier inlet “except when extremely low gas flow is set for infant,” because at flows ≤ 0.25 L/kg/min in infants a nebulizer placed proximal to the patient produced a higher inhaled dose than one placed distally (PMID 33987288). Proximal placement is also preferred in specific settings such as inspiration-synchronized delivery.
  • If using a pMDI: keep it inline with the HFNC circuit, spacer positioned close to the nasal cannula with the plume directed toward the patient (consensus Recommendation XIX). The “more than 10% at 30 L/min” figure comes from one bench study and carries three qualifiers the headline drops (Szychowiak 2021, PMID 33761286). First, it used an adult circuit and a medium-sized cannula. Second, it is not a lung dose: albuterol was caught on a filter downstream of a nasopharyngeal cast, and the authors report drug “potentially delivered to the lungs.” Third, the number was obtained only under the full optimal condition set, which includes actuating the pMDI at the beginning of inspiration (the hardest of those conditions to meet in a tachypneic infant, and one an unsynchronized actuation does not meet at all). The authors conclude that further testing in a clinical setting is required.
  • Reducing flow during nebulization is physiologically grounded and supported by the bench data, but neither its size nor its cost is usually stated, and both are large.
    Magnitude. Reaching the 0.1–0.5 GF:IF plateau in an infant supported at the measured 1.68 L/kg/min (95% CI 1.51–1.85; Milesi 2021) means cutting flow roughly 4- to 8-fold: from about 10 L/min to 1–2.5 L/min in a 5 kg infant, close to a 1:7 reduction against the 0.25 L/kg/min bench optimum. That is not trimming the flow; it is withdrawing most of the support.
    Cost. Once cannula flow drops below peak inspiratory flow you give up the nasopharyngeal dead-space washout and the flow-dependent unloading that are the indication for HFNC in the first place, and room air is entrained around the prongs, diluting the delivered FIO2. PHYSIOLOGIC REASONING. No study in this reference list measures the oxygenation or work-of-breathing cost of reducing flow for the nebulization period, so the size of that cost is unquantified here.
    This is the same trade-off as the derecruitment question in Key Principle 5, differing only in degree: taking HFNC off forfeits all of the support, turning the flow down forfeits much of it. A reader who accepts one should not treat the other as unrelated.
    Evidence. The supporting RCT is the trial that was null on its robust criterion (Key Principle 2), and the same 2023 Delphi panel withdrew its draft statement recommending flow titration below PIF across all rounds. No consensus recommendation exists. Treat it as a reasoned option, not a guideline-endorsed step.

5. For comparison, conventional routes report a range, not a single number.

  • pMDI + spacer + facemask, young children: 0.2% with a non-tight mask, 0.6% while screaming with a tight mask, and 4.8–8.2% breathing normally (Erzinger 2007); 1.97 ± 1.4% at mean age 21 months (Tal 1996); 5.4% of the metered dose in children < 4 y (Wildhaber 1999); 18.1 ± 9.1% with a modern antistatic spacer at 3–5 y (Ditcham 2014). Mask seal and behaviour dominate this number, not the device.
  • pMDI + spacer + mouthpiece: 9.6% of the metered dose in children > 4 y (Wildhaber 1999); 22.5 ± 7.9% with an antistatic spacer at 3–5 y (Ditcham 2014); in adults, 11.5% through a static spacer vs 45.6% through a detergent-coated antistatic spacer (Pierart 1999). Static charge on the spacer moves this more than the interface does.
  • Jet nebulizer, mask or mouthpiece, no HFNC: 5.4% of the nebulized dose in children < 4 y and 11.1% in children > 4 y with stable asthma (Wildhaber 1999). Lower in infants and with poor mask fit.
  • Whether to remove HFNC to nebulize is not settled in children.
    ADULT CONSENSUS The 2023 consensus states that aerosol delivery efficiency with a nebulizer via HFNC at flows ≤ 35 L/min is similar to that with a nebulizer and mask or mouthpiece, and that discontinuing HFNC to give a nebulizer by mask or mouthpiece is not recommended (very good consensus, strong recommendation); placing a mask nebulizer on a patient concurrently receiving HFNC should also be avoided. That document evaluates adult evidence only, and says so in its own limitations: the recommendations “may not apply to aerosol delivery in infants and children receiving various forms of respiratory support” (PMID 37436585).
    The pediatric bench data point in both directions. Against removal: continuous nebulization through HFNC with a vibrating mesh nebulizer delivered 14.8 ± 0.7% versus 3.2 ± 0.6% for a large-volume jet nebulizer with facemask in a pediatric manikin (Moody & Ari 2020, PMID 32681768). For removal: in a 9-month-old infant upper-airway model, deposition was greater with the HFNC taken out of the nares: 6.05 ± 1.53% (jet nebulizer) and 39.54 ± 8.98% (pMDI with valved holding chamber) without HFNC, against 2.91 ± 0.23% and 6.04 ± 0.28% with it (Alalwan 2019, PMID 31468741). This tool's own numbers carry the same asymmetry: 0.2–2.5% via HFNC in the 5–10 L/min band, against 5.4–11.1% by mask or mouthpiece in children (Wildhaber 1999).
    What is settled is the trade-off, not the answer. Alalwan's own conclusion is that any gain in delivered dose must be weighed against the change in oxygen delivery and the risk of lung derecruitment when the prongs come off. In a hypoxemic child who depends on the flow, that argues for leaving it on; in a stable child for whom a brief interruption is safe, removal may well deliver more drug. One version of this tool asserted the removal rule as a directive in one direction and a later version asserted it in the other. Neither directive is supported for children, and this bullet no longer gives one.

6. Two caveats the flow axis hides. First, HFNC is not an established bronchodilator route in pediatric critical asthma: the 2025 AARC/PALISI GRADE guideline explicitly cannot recommend for or against HFNC versus conventional oxygen (Recommendation 7) and suggests bi-level PAP over conventional oxygen (Recommendation 8). Second, in infant HFNC, circuit condensation, not flow, is a dominant loss mechanism; decoupling temperature from humidity control, and moving the nebulizer, changes the delivered dose (Zhang 2025). Two corrections to how that has been stated here before. The 11–44% figure is upper-airway deposition (drug lost proximally, not drug delivered), and it sits one paragraph from a table of 0.2–5.7% lung doses, so it must not be read as a delivered dose. In the same neonatal model the inhaled lung dose was 0.2–0.8%. And the loss is attributed to the non-heated components, not the heated circuit: DiBlasi 2024 assigns the coarse-droplet dispersion, that 11–44% deposition, and the occlusion of the supine neonatal upper airway to impaction and condensation in the non-heated HFNC and aerosol-delivery components. The mitigation therefore runs opposite to what an earlier phrasing here implied: maintaining circuit heating is what limits rainout, and turning humidification down is the wrong response.

Changes made in v3.0

The platforms are now named, because “mesh versus jet” was hiding the larger effect. A new section identifies Optiflow (Fisher & Paykel) and Vapotherm (Precision Flow / Hi-VNI / HVT 2.0), states which nebulizer each is designed for, and shows where the nebulizer sits in each circuit. This is quoted from the manufacturers’ own instructions for use rather than assumed. Both are used with the same Aerogen vibrating mesh nebulizer, and the platform difference in bench inhaled dose is larger than the mesh-versus-jet difference this tool was built around: 0.9–2.5% via Vapotherm against 12.6–18.2% via Optiflow in one adult manikin (Alanazi & Li 2023), and a ≥ 6-fold gap in another (Li 2021). Vapotherm’s IFU describes a vibrating-mesh configuration only, and no jet-through-Vapotherm study was located.

Two things the device section deliberately does not claim. That the triple-lumen delivery tube causes the gap is labelled physiologic reasoning, not a tested finding. No study here isolates the responsible component. And the aerosol gap does not extend to respiratory support: the one clinical head-to-head found no difference between the platforms in effort of breathing (p = .12, Weiler 2017).

Peak inspiratory flow now has worked cases instead of being an abstraction. Six toggleable cards span infant to adult, each tagged MEASURED, DERIVED or EXTRAPOLATED, with the derivation printed on the card and every GF:IF ratio computed in code from the stated flow rather than typed in. The 15 kg toddler card asserts no ratio, because the simulated inspiratory flow for that model could not be sourced; the adolescent card gives a range rather than a number, and shows why the weight-indexed infant constant cannot be extrapolated: carried to 50 kg it predicts 84 L/min, above every adult pattern in the literature.

A definitional hazard has been surfaced. Li 2019 computes inspiratory flow as VT/TI; Milesi 2021 measured a true spirometric peak. The two are not the same quantity and the ratios built on them are not poolable. Earlier versions used both without saying so.

Corrections made in v2.1

Population labels added where the evidence is adult-only. The instruction “do not remove HFNC in order to nebulize” rested entirely on a consensus statement that evaluates adult evidence and states in its own limitations that it may not apply to infants and children. That bullet now carries the label, reports the pediatric bench data pointing both ways (Moody & Ari 2020 against removal; Alalwan 2019 for it), and no longer issues a directive in either direction for children. The 17.2–20.8% band in the 10–20 L/min row was likewise adult in origin while sitting under a heading that read “Older child”; the pediatric values Perry 2013 measured at those same flows (0.1% and 0.0%) had been left out.

Two weight columns that answered different questions have been separated. “Typical Patient” and the DERIVED 0.25 L/kg/min column disagreed by 6- to 8-fold, and the untagged one was the column a reader indexes on. Both are now tagged, titled with the question each answers, and marked EXTRAPOLATED where the infant-measured coefficient is applied above infancy.

The 10–15 L/min crossover claim in Figure A has been withdrawn. Neither cited source measured a pediatric inspiratory flow. Measured mean peak tidal inspiratory flow in 44 infants is 7.45 L/min (Milesi 2021), so the claim was roughly twofold high for infants; for older children no measured value exists in the cited literature, so no band replaces it.

The randomized trial is now reported by both of its endpoints. The 64% vs 29% vs 27% (p=0.011) previously shown is a secondary responder definition; on the ATS/ERS criterion the authors call robust, the trial found no difference between flows at any dose (64% vs 58% vs 42%, p=0.271 at 1.5 mg). The low-ratio strategy is labelled hypothesis-generating.

Three source-fidelity errors fixed. The Li 2019 point in Chart A was plotted at 10 L/min under a tooltip describing 20 L/min; the value (20.8 ± 0.7%) is correct and correctly placed, and the tooltip now names its simulated inspiratory flow, with the two 20 L/min peaks added as their own points. The condensation caveat had assigned to the heated circuit a loss the source assigns to the non-heated components (an inversion that pointed the mitigation the wrong way), and printed an upper-airway deposition figure (11–44%) unlabelled among lung doses. PMID 33761286 was attributed to “Bennett G” and PMID 31468741 to “Ari A”; the first authors are Szychowiak and Alalwan.

The footer attestation has been narrowed from “Evidence PubMed-verified” to a statement of what was actually checked, because an attestation is what tells a reader to stop checking.

Corrections made in v2.0

“DiBlasi 2015” has been removed. Earlier versions of this tool cited it in the subtitle and footnote as a source of the delivery percentages. No DiBlasi HFNC aerosol-delivery study exists for 2015. The only 2015 DiBlasi aerosol publication is a narrative review (Respir Care 2015;60(6):894–914, PMID 26070582) that reports no HFNC albuterol delivery percentages; the actual DiBlasi HFNC study is DiBlasi 2024 (PMID 38981652), now cited throughout.

Four of five jet-nebulizer cells have been removed rather than re-estimated, and both figures have been rebuilt. Chart A previously plotted a smooth interpolated curve with no underlying data; it now plots only measured, study-attributed points. Chart B previously showed a peak at HFNC:PIF = 1.0; the published relationship is monotonically decreasing with a plateau below 0.5, so the peak, its dashed “HFNC = PIF” optimum marker, and the “air entrainment dilutes the aerosol” explanation for the sub-1 region have all been replaced.
